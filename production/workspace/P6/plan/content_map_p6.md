## CHAPTER 1: PHOTOSYNTHESIS (Energy, pp. 2–17)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
What do living things need to carry out life processes? · Where does the energy in living things come from? · How is energy from the Sun used by plants? · How do plants and animals obtain energy from food? Syllabus: Sun as primary source; requirements for photosynthesis (water, light energy, carbon dioxide); energy from respiration (Standard only; focus on releasing energy from food).

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Opener (p. 2):** the cast argue over the most important life process. The book's definition of photosynthesis: plants use water, carbon dioxide and light to make sugar and oxygen; chlorophyll, usually in leaves, absorbs the light (1.1–1.4).
2. **Energy for life processes (pp. 3–5):** moving, growing, reproducing, even resting. Plants get energy from their sugar, animals from food. Calories; carbohydrates, proteins, fats; nutrition labels and a balanced diet (1.5–1.13).
3. **The Sun is the primary source (pp. 6–7):** its heat keeps the Earth warm; its light drives photosynthesis; stored energy passes to animals. Sun → grass → rabbit → fox, before the term *food chain* (1.14–1.19).
4. **Photosynthesis in the leaf (pp. 8–10):** chlorophyll traps light; leaves take in carbon dioxide and give out oxygen through tiny openings; light needs differ. Cactus question (answered in Ch 5). I'm Inspired: van Helmont's willow (p. 9). Excess sugar → starch, stored in leaves, roots, stems; iodine brown → blue-black; potatoes (stems) and sweet potatoes (roots) (1.20–1.24c).
5. **Respiration (pp. 11–12):** releases energy from food, needs oxygen, gives carbon dioxide and water. Plants respire all the time. Comparison table: photosynthesis (plants, only in light, carbon dioxide in, oxygen out) vs respiration (plants and animals, all the time, oxygen in, carbon dioxide out) (1.25–1.30).
6. **Let's Apply (pp. 13–16):** bubbles (oxygen) vs torch distance; destarched leaf with black paper; carbon dioxide and rate; why a dark room fails.

### Key terms
life processes, energy, calories, primary source of energy, directly / indirectly, photosynthesis, chlorophyll, carbon dioxide, sugar, oxygen, tiny openings, starch, iodine solution, respiration, respire.

### Singapore examples
More than 90% of food imported; the "30 by 30" goal (p. 6). Food logs to track calories (p. 4).

### Misconceptions / exam points
- **Plants respire all the time** (the book's comic, p. 11).
- Photosynthesis makes **sugar**; starch is the stored excess (app mix-up 41).
- A plant **makes** food and **gives out** oxygen (mix-up 43). Answer keys: not "nutrients" for food (Set 1 Q34b); say the food is *produced by the leaves* (Set 1 Q34c).
- Recitation lines needing care: respiration listed as a use of energy (1.5); *glucose* and word equations (1.2, 1.25); trophic levels and heat loss along food chains (1.36–1.37).

### Takeaways and WIHL
Takeaways pp. 5, 7, 10, 12 (energy for life processes; Sun as primary source; light, water, carbon dioxide → sugar, oxygen; energy from food through respiration). WIHL p. 17 repeats them (1.34).

## CHAPTER 2: ENERGY CONVERSION (Energy, pp. 18–45) — Standard only

### Key questions / learning outcomes
What is energy? · What are the different forms of energy? · What is energy conversion? · Where does the energy we use come from? · How can we help to conserve energy? Syllabus: forms of energy with examples; investigate conversion. **The terms chemical, gravitational and elastic potential energy are not required.**

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Energy (pp. 18–19):** required for things to work (the monograph's takeaway, word for word); seen only by its effects (2.1–2.4).
2. **Forms (pp. 20–28),** identified by motion, position and interactions:
   - **Kinetic energy** (pp. 20–22): a moving object's energy; more with more speed, and with more mass at the same speed (full vs empty bus).
   - **Potential energy** (pp. 23–26): stored energy due to position or state, in three kinds the book also names: due to height ("gravitational"; more height or mass → more), due to stretching or compressing ("elastic"), in chemicals ("chemical": batteries, food, fuels).
   - **Light** (allows us to see), **electrical** (in a closed circuit), **sound** (allows us to hear), **heat** (related to changes in temperature) (pp. 27–28).
   - Takeaway: six forms (2.23).
3. **Conversion (pp. 29–32):** battery + bulb (potential → electrical → light and heat); falling apple and slingshot (potential → kinetic); radio (electrical → sound); Let's Explore: cyclist, kettle, ramp, thrown ball (2.24–2.29).
4. **Sources (pp. 32–35):** fossil fuels hold energy from the Sun; burnt for electrical and kinetic energy; non-renewable. Alternatives (moving water, wind, solar) all trace to the Sun (2.30–2.38).
5. **Conserving energy (pp. 36–37):** use energy efficiently, reduce wastage; six habits; Science Around Us: renewable energy and its limits, nuclear energy, biofuels (2.39–2.44).
6. **Let's Apply (pp. 38–43):** wind-up toy; skateboard ramp; hanging balls of different mass; rubber-band jumper; wind turbine blade length.

### Key terms
energy, kinetic energy, potential energy (stored energy), light / electrical / sound / heat energy, closed circuit, energy conversion, fossil fuels, non-renewable, renewable, alternative sources, solar panels, conserve, efficiently, wastage, sustainably, depleted.

### Singapore examples
95% of Singapore's electricity comes from natural gas (p. 34). Habits: fan instead of air conditioner, public transport, insulating materials against daytime heat gain (p. 36).

### Misconceptions / exam points
- **Energy is not "used up".** The book's own trap (Q3, p. 39): kinetic energy was **converted to other forms**. Set 2 Q39b rejects "EE used up"; mix-up 33.
- **Name forms exactly:** "potential energy" alone earns the mark (Set 1 Q41a); not "gravitational energy" (mix-up 35), not "chemical energy" (Set 2 Q39a), not "electricity" (mix-up 18).
- **Has vs converts:** list forms when asked what an object *has* (mix-up 36).
- Mass comparisons hold only **at the same speed / height** (pp. 22, 24). Kinetic energy depending on mass and speed **is in the textbook**.

### Takeaways and WIHL
Takeaways pp. 28, 32, 35, 37 (six forms; conversion; energy from the Sun; use energy efficiently and sustainably). WIHL p. 44 (the last as "We should use…"); concept map p. 45 (2.48–2.49).

## CHAPTER 3: INTERACTION OF FORCES (Interactions, pp. 48–73)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
What is a force? · What are the effects of forces? · What are the different types of forces? Syllabus: effects of a force; frictional force and reducing it; gravitational force and weight; elastic spring force (**Standard only**).

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Force (pp. 48–50):** a push or a pull, the action when objects interact; "exerted on X by Y"; some forces act without contact (3.1–3.5).
2. **Effects (pp. 50–52):** change shape; move a stationary object; speed up, slow down or stop; change direction (3.6–3.9).
3. **Magnetic force (pp. 52–53):** by magnets on magnetic materials and magnets; attraction and repulsion; no contact needed (3.11–3.13).
4. **Frictional force (pp. 54–59):** opposes motion when objects rub; box, brakes, swimmer, feather. I'm Inspired: Galileo (p. 56); streamlined racing car. Useful (grip; less on smooth surfaces; oily hands) and a problem (wear and tear; heat in blenders and drills); reduced by lubricants and wheels; turns kinetic energy into heat (3.14–3.26).
5. **Gravitational force (pp. 60–64):** the Earth's pull on all objects, all the time; tides; weight; less weight in space; mass vs weight; satellites (3.27–3.34).
6. **Elastic spring force (pp. 65–67):** push or pull of a stretched or compressed object; restores its shape; harder to stretch, more force; trampoline, spring rider (3.35–3.39).
7. **Let's Apply (pp. 68–71):** not a force (gaining heat); biscuit in tongs; block sliding to a stop; spring length vs mass; launcher on two surfaces.

### Key terms
force, push, pull, exert, contact, stationary, direction, magnetic force, attraction, repulsion, frictional force, rough / smooth, streamlined, wear and tear, lubricant, gravitational force, weight, mass, matter, tides, elastic spring force, stretched, compressed.

### Real-world examples
No Singapore-specific example in pp. 48–73. Contexts: school field, playground, bicycle brakes, kitchen appliances, anti-slip strips, satellites.

### Misconceptions / exam points
- A rising ball moves **against** gravitational force, not "overcoming gravity" (mix-up 27); gravity acts on it while it rises (p. 60).
- Springs stretch, extend, compress; not expand/contract (mix-up 28). *Stiffer*, not *stronger* (mix-up 19; Set 2 Q41b(i)).
- Name both surfaces and say *how* friction is reduced (mix-up 32; Set 1 Q40d). Extra wrong forces lose marks (Set 2 Q41a).
- Let's Apply Q3: expected answer (2), friction at A (moving) not at B (stopped); gravity at both.
- Recitation terms not in the book: *accelerate*, *air resistance*, *support force*, *ball bearings*, friction rising with mass (3.17–3.48).

### Takeaways and WIHL
Takeaways pp. 50, 52, 67 (push or pull; four effects; four types). WIHL p. 72; concept map p. 73 (3.44–3.45).

## CHAPTER 4: INTERACTIONS WITHIN THE ENVIRONMENT (Interactions, pp. 74–87) ⚠ textbook unread

### Key questions / learning outcomes (contents, textbook ✓)
What makes up an environment? · What affects the survival of an organism in an environment? · Where do different organisms live? · How do organisms in a community interact to stay alive? Syllabus: habitats, populations, communities; food chains and food webs.

### Core concepts (likely order: TLG key ideas 1–4; AB/labs 4.1–4.4)
1. **Environment (⚠):** living things interacting with other factors; opener a mangrove swamp (TLG).
2. **Factors (⚠):** temperature, light, water; food; other organisms (producers, consumers, decomposers).
3. **Habitats, populations, communities (⚠):** garden, field, pond, seashore, tree, mangrove swamp; population and community Standard only.
4. **Food chains and webs (p. 84 partly read):** populations in a habitat depend on one another for energy; duckweed → water snail → frog; a chain begins with a producer; consumers, predator, prey; food webs Standard only (4.10–4.17).
5. **Decomposers (⚠):** return nutrients to the soil.

### Key terms
environment, organism, habitat, population, community, interdependence, food chain, food web, producer, consumer, predator, prey, decomposer.

### Singapore examples (⚠)
Mangrove shore (p. 110 picture); school habitats (TLG, labs).

### Misconceptions / exam points
- Not every consumer is a predator (TLG; the water snail, p. 84).
- Arrows point from food to feeder (lab 1.5). A prey population **decreases**, not "becomes extinct" (lab 4.4).
- Decomposers make **simpler** (not smaller) substances (Set 1 Q35c(ii)).
- Check when readable: habitat "natural" (4.5), population "species" (4.8), predator "organism" vs the book's "animal" (4.14).

## CHAPTER 5: SURVIVING IN THE ENVIRONMENT (Interactions, pp. 88–109) ⚠ textbook unread

### Key questions / learning outcomes (contents, textbook ✓)
How do organisms survive well in an environment? · How do the actions of humans affect the environment? Syllabus: adaptations for survival; man's impact on the environment.

### Core concepts (likely order: TLG key ideas 5–6; AB/labs 5.1–5.3)
1. **Adaptations (⚠):** structural (beaks, waxy leaves, needle-like leaves) and behavioural (night activity, hiding in a shell, migration), for coping with physical factors, food, escaping predators, reproducing; opener a camouflage scene (TLG).
2. **Human impact (p. 100 partly read):** negative: depletion of natural resources, pollution, deforestation, global warming; positive: conservation, reforestation, buying only what we need, reuse, recycling (5.8–5.11; labs 5.2–5.3).

### Key terms
adaptation, structural, behavioural, camouflage, migration; natural resources, depletion, pollution, deforestation, global warming, conservation, endangered, reforestation, recycle.

### Singapore examples
Zero Waste Nation recycling bins (p. 100, textbook ✓). TLG only (⚠): Sungei Buloh migratory birds; Sisters' Islands turtle hatchery (2018).

### Misconceptions / exam points
- Name the behaviour or feature, then how it helps (Set 1 Q36a: hide inside the shell, not just "protect"; Set 2 Q36b: evidence, camouflage, "harder to be spotted").
- Recitation Ch 6 includes past-paper contexts beyond the syllabus (disease carriers, more females hatching, pesticides) and "particles" (haze) (5.20–5.24).

## 3. LEVEL NOTES

### Standard vs Foundation
Standard book. Standard only: Energy Conversion (all of Ch 2), elastic spring force, respiration; population, community and food webs (TLG).

### Accuracy flags (collected; check before every script)
1. **Forms of energy (serious).** Book: six forms, potential energy in three named kinds. Recitation: "eight forms". Syllabus: kind names not required. BRIEF-P6: kind always required. App poster: separate "Chemical energy", "Heat (thermal)". **Rule:** six forms; describe the kind in the book's plain words; the kind name only as "also called…"; never "chemical energy" or "thermal".
2. **"Energy cannot be created or destroyed" (serious):** recitation and app poster; not in the book, monograph or syllabus. Use "converted to other forms".
3. **Energy definition:** book and monograph "required for things to work"; recitation "ability to do work" (beyond the syllabus).
4. **Kinetic energy and mass/speed:** in the textbook (pp. 20–22); safe.
5. **Electrical, sound, heat definitions:** recitation's *electric charges*, *vibrating*, *hotter to cooler* vs the book's *closed circuit*, *allows us to hear*, *changes in temperature*. Prefer the book.
6. **Glucose and word equations** (Recitation Ch 1): not in the book; say sugar.
7. **Respiration as a use of energy** (Recitation Ch 1) vs the book's moving, growing, reproducing.
8. **Trophic levels; heat loss along food chains:** not in pp. 2–73; trophic level is not a syllabus term.
9. **Gravitational force (serious):** recitation "attraction between objects with mass", "accelerate downward" vs the book's "pull that the Earth exerts on all objects"; acceleration is on the BRIEF-P6 Never list.
10. **Not in the book:** friction rising with mass, ball bearings, support force, air resistance (accepted in answer keys; narrate "frictional force exerted by the air").
11. **Friction → heat** (book) vs **heat and sound** (recitation, keys); both fine in explanations.
12. **Magnetic force** "between magnets or magnetic materials" (recitation) could imply two non-magnets attract; use the book.
13. **Habitat / population / predator** wording differs (recitation vs TLG vs book p. 84).
14. **Let's Apply p. 15 Q3:** parts P and R not described; answer unconfirmed.
15. **Van Helmont** spelled "Jon Baptist" (p. 9); say "van Helmont". **Calories** (p. 4) are food-label calories.
16. **App wording (outside this plan):** Forces poster "squashed spring" (book: compressed); lab 3.7 note on a rail and gravitational pull is muddled; lab 1.4 "stored starch is given out" (say used).

### Negative-word watch
Book: fossil-fuel organisms "died" (p. 33); forests "destroyed" (p. 100). Recitation: "dead organisms", "causing organisms to die", "Habitat destroyed". TLG: "or die". Narrate "remains of plants and animals", "fewer survive", "habitats are cleared". Science terms stay: extinct, predator, prey, decomposer, endangered.
