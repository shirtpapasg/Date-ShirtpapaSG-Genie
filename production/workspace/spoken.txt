If you live in Singapore, you have breathed this smoke. What is it costing your lungs, and why does it never stop coming back?
In the next few minutes, we will follow this smoke backwards, from your window to the fire that made it, and find the one point on that path where you can step in.
First, what are you actually breathing? Haze is mostly tiny particles called PM2.5. That means particles smaller than two point five micrometres, about thirty times thinner than a human hair. They are small enough to slip past your nose and throat and travel deep into your lungs, and some can even pass into your blood. That is why your eyes sting, why people with asthma struggle, and why NEA tells us to cut down on outdoor activity when the PSI climbs.
So where does it come from? Not from Singapore. During the dry season, fires burn across parts of Sumatra and Kalimantan in Indonesia, and sometimes in Malaysia. From around June to October, the southwest monsoon winds carry that smoke across the region, straight to us. The wind is the delivery truck. It is not the cause.
Here is the part most people never learn. Many of these fires are not burning in normal soil. They burn in peat. Peat is a thick layer of dead plants that has built up over thousands of years in waterlogged swamps. Because it is always wet, the plants never fully rot. They just pile up, storing huge amounts of carbon.
But when companies or farmers dig canals to drain the swamp for plantations, the peat dries out. And dry peat is basically fuel. Once it catches fire, it does not just burn on the surface. It smoulders underground, sometimes metres deep, for weeks. Water bombing from helicopters barely touches it. And because it burns slowly with little oxygen, the combustion is incomplete, which is exactly the kind of burning that produces the most smoke.
Now add the weather. In El Niño years, the region gets drier for longer, so the peat dries out more and fires spread more easily. That is why some years are far worse than others.
So why start the fires at all? Because burning is the cheapest and fastest way to clear land. Clearing by machine costs money. A match does not. Much of that cleared land becomes plantations for palm oil, and for pulp and paper.
Which brings us back to you. Palm oil is in a huge range of products: instant noodles, biscuits, chocolate, shampoo, soap, even lipstick. Paper products like tissue and printing paper can come from pulpwood plantations too. Every purchase is a small vote for how that product was made.
So here is the step you can take. You do not need to boycott all palm oil. Oil palm actually produces more oil per hectare than most other oil crops, so swapping it for something else can mean clearing even more land. Instead, choose products from companies that have committed to no burning and no deforestation. Look for the RSPO label, which certifies sustainable palm oil, and the FSC label on paper and tissue. In Singapore, the Singapore Green Labelling Scheme also marks products that meet these standards. And if a brand you love does not say where its palm oil comes from, ask them. Companies respond when customers ask.
Singapore has also taken action. The Transboundary Haze Pollution Act lets Singapore hold companies responsible for haze that reaches us, even if the fire happened outside our borders. Laws work best when consumers back them up with their wallets.
So, that is the full path: a drained peat swamp, an underground fire, the monsoon wind, and PM2.5 in your lungs. And that is your step on that path: the shopping basket.
