**Why two parts, not one.** I first laid the whole special out as ONE part with every ritual the rulebook asks for (a voice in the first second, the hidden animal, goals, two rounds, two look-closers, a HOLD ON!, the journey, the app walk-through, the week of reviews, the chant, the read-along, "Now you know!"): it came to **about 8:44**, far over the 5:15 to 5:46 the series allows a part. Cutting it to one part would have meant dropping the app walk-through or the week of reviews, and those are exactly the two things the old video teaches that a child cannot get from the textbook. The content splits cleanly in two: **Part 1 is "why"** (make it yourself; work it out; memory first) and ends on a lock-in a child can only answer by watching the week; **Part 2 is "how"** (make one in TheOneApp; check and fix; space it out) and answers that lock-in with two blank pages. Each part has its own goals, score and key words (rulebook section 2). If the teacher prefers one part anyway, section 11 choice 1 says what a 5:45 one-part cut would lose.

## 2. The chain of questions, the spine and the split

**The chain (each answer opens the next question; SERIES_FORMAT 1c):**
1. *Pip reads a ready-made mind map; Basil makes his own. One week later, who will remember more?* → (held open to the end of Part 2.) First, a smaller test the viewer can do right now:
2. *The word puzzle: three words you read, three you work out. Which will you remember better?* → (locked in; answered after the look-closer, so there is time to forget.)
3. *What is different about reading a map and making one?* → **Up close: Pip's eyes slide over the poster; Basil stops, thinks and chooses his own words.** Making your own map means you work out every branch yourself.
4. *The memory test: which puzzle words are still there?* → **The worked-out ones, for many people; scientists have tested it many times: the generation effect.** Answer: 2.
5. *Momo "makes her own" too, by copying the poster word for word. Is that making your own?* (HOLD ON!) → **Copying is not remembering. Close the poster, draw what you remember first.** Memory first.
6. *Next Monday, a blank page each: who draws more?* (Part 1's lock-in) → Part 2: first, how does Basil make his?
7. *How do you make one in TheOneApp, and how do you know what you missed?* → **Poster closed, from memory; then Mark my mind map; Compare with the poster; add the missing idea in your own words.** Check and fix.
8. *Pip's steps poster: "tap Compare with the poster first, and copy it"?* (Showdown) → **No: that is copying. Memory first, then compare.**
9. *Why draw it again on Tuesday and Thursday, and not just read it five times tonight?* (be an idea) → **Each time you pull an idea out after a gap, its path gets clearer.** Space it out: one day, three days, a week.
10. *Next Monday: two blank pages* → **Pip two branches, Basil all five.** → the viewer's dare: make your own tonight, draw it again tomorrow.

**The spine:** a one-week memory test (EP1's 30-day box, shortened to a week), set at the void deck study corner, with a ONE-WEEK MEMORY TEST card on the notice board that fills day by day and a day counter in Part 2.

**Why the split is here:** the old video's own order. Part 1 is its first half (which map helps you remember more; the word puzzle; the reader or the maker; memory first) and ends on the big question. Part 2 is its second half (make a map in TheOneApp; make one together; memory first, then check; review it again) and answers the big question.

- **Facts to check (web, standard search, 24 Nov 2026; how sure I am of each, as worded):**
  1. **The generation effect** (spoken: "Scientists have tested this many times, with grown-ups and with children, and the worked-out words are remembered better."): very sure. Slamecka and Graf (1978) named it; a meta-analysis of 86 studies (445 effect sizes) found an average advantage of d = 0.40 for generated over read items (Bertsch, Pesta, Wiscott and McDaniel, 2007, *Memory & Cognition*, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/); it is larger in cued recall (d about 0.55) than free recall; a later meta-analysis (McCurdy et al., 2020, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-020-01762-3, 310 experiments) confirms it. **In children:** it has been found from about age 7 (McFarland, Duncan and Bruno, 1983, cited in later reviews), and self-generated learning helps school-age children (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4919217). **Honest limits:** it is an average, not a promise for every child or every word; the in-video puzzle is a demonstration with six well-known words, so a child may remember all six: Heart says "Many people do", never "you did".
  2. **A map you make beats a map you only read** (Mark Sentence 1): sure, with a caveat. A meta-analysis of concept-map studies (Schroeder, Nesbit, Anguiano and Adesope, 2018, *Educational Psychology Review* 30, 431-455, https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-017-9403-9; 142 effect sizes, 11,814 learners) found that **creating** concept maps helped learning more (g = 0.72) than **studying** ready-made ones (g = 0.43), each against its own comparison group (so this is not one head-to-head test). Together with the generation effect, "helps you remember more than a map you only read" is fair; no number is spoken.
  3. **Memory first, then check** (the HOLD ON! and Mark Sentence 2 of Part 1): very sure, and the HOLD ON! is built on it. Students who practised **retrieval** (writing what they remembered, text closed) learned more than students who made a concept map **with the text open** (Karpicke and Blunt, 2011, *Science* 331, 772-775, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21252317/; the What Works Clearinghouse review gives 67% vs 45% correct one week later, college students, https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/67306), and making the concept map **from memory** works as a retrieval practice (Blunt and Karpicke, 2014, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/Blunt2014.pdf). So Momo's copied map is the weaker way, and Basil's poster-closed map the stronger. **In children:** retrieval practice effects are robust in primary-school children (Karpicke, Blunt and Smith, 2016, *Frontiers in Psychology*, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786565), and younger children benefit most when retrieval is guided, e.g. by a map's structure (Karpicke, Blunt, Smith and Karpicke, 2014, *JARMAC*, https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2014/2014_Karpicke_etal_JARMAC.pdf): TheOneApp's chapter, middle word and branch boxes are that guide. Testing beats re-reading after a week (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006, *Psychological Science*, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/; the university's press release gives 61% vs 40% after a week, https://source.washu.edu/2006/04/repeated-testing-better-than-repeated-studying/), and re-reading feels easier and gives more confidence than it earns (same study): Pip's "Reading feels safe!" and "it felt easy, but it did not stick" are the true point. **Checking after recall** (feedback) helps: very sure. Practice testing and distributed practice are the two techniques rated "high utility" in Dunlosky et al. (2013, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky).
  4. **One day, three days, a week** (Mark Sentence 2 of Part 2, the chant, the dare): **the spacing is very sure; the exact days are a simple plan, not a proven best schedule.** Spreading reviews out beats doing them all at once (Dunlosky et al. 2013, above); the best gap grows with how long you need to remember, but not by a fixed rule (Cepeda et al., 2008, *Psychological Science*); growing gaps (1, 3, 7 days) and equal gaps work about equally well for long-term memory in the studies so far (e.g. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19966244/). The old video's "one day, three days and a week" is kept because it is simple, true as advice and spreads the reviews out; the teacher sheet says any spread-out days work. Section 11 choice 6 and open point 3.
  5. **Pip's two branches against Basil's five** (Part 2 Round 1): a story outcome, not data. It is in the direction the studies show (re-reading all at once vs retrieval spaced over days); no number is said aloud apart from the branches on the two drawn pages.
  6. **The memory garden** (journey): a picture, not a fact. What is true at this level: remembering something again after a gap makes it easier to remember next time, and an idea you never pull out again gets harder to find. The journey ends with "There is no real garden in your head. It is a picture of what scientists found." No brain parts are named (no "neurons", "brain cells", "connections").
  7. **Crows remember faces for years** (Momo, Part 1): very sure for American crows. In Seattle, researchers wore a "dangerous" mask while trapping and ringing a few crows (2006); years later crows still scolded that mask, including crows that were never caught (Marzluff and colleagues; Cornell, Marzluff and Pecoraro, 2012, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*); scolding was still seen years later and had faded by about 17 years (https://urban.uw.edu/news/crows-hold-grudges-against-individual-humans-for-up-to-17-years; https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna43570637). The line says "crows" and "Scientists wore masks near crows", true as said; it does not claim the house crows of Singapore were tested (about 80% sure they can do the same, not said). The mask is shown as a plain cartoon mask (no real person's face).
  8. **A pet cat can learn its own name, even from a stranger's voice** (Momo, Part 2): sure. Cats from homes and from a cat café told their own name apart from other nouns, including when an unfamiliar person said it, though less strongly (Saito, Shinozuka, Ito and Hasegawa, 2019, *Scientific Reports*, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6449508). **The science, for the teacher (section 3, facts 1 to 4, with sources):** the generation effect (Bertsch et al. 2007: 86 studies, average d = 0.40); creating concept maps helps more than studying ready-made ones (Schroeder et al. 2018: g = 0.72 vs 0.43, each against its own comparison group); retrieval beats concept mapping with the text open, and mapping from memory is itself retrieval practice (Karpicke and Blunt 2011; Blunt and Karpicke 2014); retrieval practice works in primary-school children, best when guided (Karpicke et al. 2014, 2016); practice testing and spaced practice are the two "high utility" techniques (Dunlosky et al. 2013). **One day, three days and a week is a simple plan, not a magic schedule:** spacing is what matters; growing and equal gaps both work.

**Careful:** never promise a child that they will remember more; the puzzle is a demonstration. Re-reading feels easy and makes pupils confident; that feeling is not memory (Roediger and Karpicke 2006). TheOneApp's marker can be unsure ("? I am not sure about this one. Ask your teacher."): that is by design.

## 12. Choices I made

1. **Two parts** (section "Why two parts"). A one-part cut to about 5:45 would have to drop the app walk-through or the week of reviews, plus the HOLD ON! or the journey: the old video's most useful parts.
2. **The story is a one-week test**, because the old video's question ("which map helps you remember more?") is about time: the answer only shows after days, so a day counter and two blank pages show it honestly.
3. **The word puzzle is the viewer's own test**, kept from the old video, with magnet words the child met in EP19 to EP22 so the puzzle also revises; Heart never claims the child remembered more ("Many people do").
4. **The journey is a picture of memory**, said to be a picture twice (small print and Heart's last line), because the series' journeys go into real micro-worlds and memory has none a P3 child can be shown truthfully. Open point 4.
5. **The HOLD ON! is copying a map with the poster open**, because the research's sharpest finding is exactly this: making a concept map with the text open is beaten by retrieval (Karpicke and Blunt 2011), while making it from memory is retrieval (Blunt and Karpicke 2014). It turns "make your own" into "make your own, from memory".
6. **"One day, three days and a week"** is kept from the old video (simple, spreads reviews out, already in the old description), with the honest note that the days are a plan, not a rule.
7. **The worked example is Magnets**, the theme the series has just taught (EP19 to EP22), on the app's own Magnets poster; the missing branch Basil adds is *Making a magnet*, the newest idea (EP22), the one a child is most likely to forget.
8. **Scientists' names once each** (generation effect on the shelf; retrieval practice and spaced practice said by Heart): the old video named the generation effect, and P5/P6 viewers can use the terms.
9. **Hidden animals with a memory link:** a crow (remembers faces for years) and a community cat (a pet cat learns its own name); both live at void decks, neither used before.
10. **The same void deck in both parts**, because the test is fair only if it happens in the same place; the time of day, the weather and the life around it change.
