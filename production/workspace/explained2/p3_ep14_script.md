## 2. The spine and the split

EP13 asked: the Sun; every evening it goes, every morning it comes back; why: the Sun moves away, the Earth turns, or clouds hide the Sun? **Part 1** is in an HDB flat, by the living-room window, just before seven in the morning. The dare is played as Round 1 (Momo has seen the Sun move across the sky; Pip remembers a cloudy night; Basil's globe spins). Then the sky from the window is looked at closely over two days, speeded up: bright, dark, bright, dark, and on a clear night with no clouds there is still no Sun. So why does it repeat? On the table a globe is our Earth and a torch is our Sun, with a red dot on Singapore: the torch stays still, the globe turns, and the red dot goes into the light, into the dark, and into the light again. The answer is two: the Earth turns, and scientists call this turning rotation. Momo sees it: the Sun only looks as if it moves. The child becomes the little red dot and lives one full turn. The book's words: the repeated change between day and night is caused by the rotation of the Earth; this rotation takes 24 hours. Day, night, day, night: a repeated pattern of change has a name, a cycle, and the board CYCLE CHECK gets its two ticks. Basil tries his pencil (it changes, so it is a cycle!) and the child stops him: it does not repeat. Pip finds one that does, down at the junction: green, amber, red, green again. Then Momo asks the question the book's sentence leaves open: the Earth is non-living; do living things have cycles too? **Part 2** is in a community garden beside the block. An old photo from a Tree Planting Day long ago: a boy, a young tree, a teddy bear. Today the boy is a man and the young tree is a tall tree with fruits; the teddy bear is the same size. Living things grow. Then one chick is followed week after week: it grows into an adult chicken, and it has chicks of its own. Living things reproduce and have young. The book's words: cycles in living things happen when living things grow and have young. The child becomes a chick, from inside the egg to the new egg, and the ring on the board closes: egg, chick, adult chicken, and egg again: a life cycle. Pip's notes hold the exam trap (a life cycle ends at the adult). Three chickens stand together, a grandmother hen, a mother hen and a chick, and the book says why it matters: life cycles ensure that living things of the same kind can continue to live on Earth. The chant says both rings, and the dare hands over to a mango seed.

**Why the split is here:** p. 73 itself has two halves, and the plan row's big idea names them in brackets: "day and night; life cycles". Part 1 is the non-living half and two of the plan row's three goals; it answers the dare from start to finish, so a child who watches only Part 1 has the whole answer. Part 2 is the living half and the third goal, with the chapter's first two pages (pp. 74-75). Each part has its own question and its own evidence.
