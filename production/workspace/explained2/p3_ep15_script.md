## 2. The chain of questions, the spine and the split

**The chain (each answer opens the next question; SERIES_FORMAT 1c):**
1. *EP14's dare:* we plant a mango's one big seed. What will it grow into first: a mango, a flower or a young plant? → **We cannot guess it: we have to watch it.**
2. *How will we ever know? A seed grows so slowly.* → **We observe it like a scientist: again and again, with our senses, and we write it down.** (Momo's plan, "once now and once when it is a tree", would miss everything in between.)
3. *So what do we see, day after day?* → **A root comes out first and grows down; a shoot pushes up; red leaves open and turn green. The seed grew into a young plant.** (The answer is three.)
4. *How tall is it now? How do we measure it fairly?* → **With a ruler, its zero on the soil, the same way every time.** (Basil measured from the table.)
5. *Our young plant has no flowers and no mangoes. Will it ever grow mangoes?* → (Part 2) **The old mango tree downstairs grew from a seed too. It is an adult plant: adults keep growing, but only the adult plant has flowers and fruits.**
6. *Then where do new mango seeds come from?* → **Not from the roots: be a mango flower. The flower grows into a mango, and a new seed grows inside it.**
7. *So which comes first, the mango or the seed?* → **Neither: seed, young plant, adult plant, and the fruit's seeds start again. Three stages, and no first or last stage.** (Momo's homework said "the adult plant is the last stage".)
8. *What if a kind of plant had no more seeds at all?* → **Life cycles repeat to ensure that living things continue to live; with no seeds, that plant could disappear. So Singapore keeps a seed bank.**
9. *And animals?* → **There are tadpoles in the garden pond. What will a tadpole grow into?** (EP16's dare: the story carries on in the same garden.)

**The spine:** one mango seed. The cold open lifts it out of a mango in an HDB kitchen; it is planted on the corridor ledge, watched through a window in its pot and measured; in Part 2 an adult carries the young plant downstairs and sets it beside the old mango tree in the community garden, the adult plant it will one day become. The reveal is the loop board closing when a new mango is cut open and a new seed is inside.

**Why the split is here:** the book's own pages split the same way: observing and the seed becoming a young plant (pp. 76-77), then the adult plant, the three stages, the repeated pattern and the seed bank (pp. 77-79). Part 1 ends on the gap a child sees for themselves (no flowers, no mangoes yet); Part 2 fills it.

- **Realism** (brief; true at P3): the mango seed is large, flat and oval, a hairy husk; when it grows, **the root comes out first and grows down**, then a shoot comes up bent like a hook and straightens; **new mango leaves are reddish-bronze, soft and drooping, and turn green** as they harden; the old seed stays at the base of the stem for weeks. - **The hidden animals** are small but findable, in a new place in each scene, never in a [LOOK CLOSER] or a journey; both free and never touched.
- **The fun facts (checked):** (1) **Snails** have a ribbon-like tongue (a radula) covered in rows of tiny teeth, thousands of them (a garden snail about 14,000 in common sources; the script says only "thousands", which is true for garden snails); they scrape up soft leaves and are well-known pests of seedlings. (2) **Weaver ants** (*Oecophylla smaragdina*, common on Singapore's trees, mango trees among them) build nests by pulling leaves together; workers hold larvae to the leaf edges and the larvae give out the silk that binds them: the thread really comes from their babies. They are kept by mango and citrus growers in Asia and Australia to guard the fruit from pests (left out of the spoken line for length; it is in the description idea list). Both checked from established natural-history knowledge (no web access in this session); flag if the teacher wants a source.
