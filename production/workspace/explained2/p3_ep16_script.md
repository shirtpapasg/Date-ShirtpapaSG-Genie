## 2. The chain of questions, the spine and the split

**The chain (each answer opens the next question; SERIES_FORMAT 1c):**
1. *EP15's dare:* a tadpole lives in water and has a tail. What will it grow into: a fish, a frog or a lizard? → **Watch it from the very start.**
2. *Where do the tadpoles come from?* → **From eggs, laid in the water in a clump of jelly.** (Momo thought a frog has babies the way a cat has kittens.)
3. *Why are the eggs inside slippery jelly?* → **The jelly-like covering protects the eggs from being eaten and keeps them from drying out.**
4. *So what does the tadpole become?* → (be a frog egg) **It hatches, grows back legs, then front legs, its tail shrinks away, and it climbs out: a frog.** The tadpole is the young; it does not look like the adult.
5. *Is a tadpole with legs a new stage?* → **No: it is still the young. Egg, young, adult: three stages, round a loop.** (Pip counted four.)
6. *Does every young animal look so different from its parents?* → **No: compare the body parts, not the colour. A chick looks like the adult.** Some young look like their parents, some do not. (Basil compared only the colour.)
7. *A little grasshopper with no wings, and an empty grasshopper on the grass: what happened?* → (Part 2) **The little one is the young, a nymph: it looks like the adult, but has no wings yet.** (Basil: "it is just a tiny adult".)
8. *How does a nymph grow into an adult with wings, and what is the empty grasshopper?* → **Its hard outer skin cannot stretch; it sheds it and grows a new one, several times: moulting. The last time, it has wings.** (Momo: "it dried up".)
9. *Do other insects have nymphs?* → **The cockroach: eggs in an egg case, a nymph that looks like the adult, three stages.** (Pip's homework said its young does not look like the adult.)
10. *And a caterpillar on the lime plant: is it a nymph too?* → EP17's dare: it does not look like a butterfly at all... (EP17: a larva, and four stages).

**The spine:** one place, our block's community garden (EP15's), visited from dawn to mid-morning. The pond gives the frog's whole cycle; the grassy edge gives the hen and chicks, the little grasshopper and its empty skin; the bench and the easels are where the buddies work it out; the lime plant at the edge carries the story into EP17.

**Why the split is here:** the book's own order: the chicken and the frog (pp. 82-83), then the grasshopper, moulting and the cockroach (pp. 84-85). Part 1 ends on what a child would find next in the same grass (a wingless grasshopper and its empty skin); Part 2 explains it.

- **The fun facts (checked against established natural history; no web access in this session):**
  1. **The four-lined tree frog** (*Polypedates leucomystax*), one of the commonest frogs in Singapore's gardens, lays its eggs in a **foam nest**: the pair beat a sticky liquid into froth with their back legs, usually on a leaf or plant over the water or at its edge; when the tadpoles hatch, they drop or wriggle down into the water. **How sure: high (about 90%)**: foam nests are the textbook trait of this genus; the one soft point is "on a leaf above the pond" (some nests are at the water's edge or on a pot rim), which is why the line says "this tree frog... on a leaf above the pond", i.e. the one we show.
  2. **The praying mantis is a close relative of the cockroach** (mantises, cockroaches and termites form one group of insects, Dictyoptera); like a cockroach, a mother mantis lays her eggs inside an **egg case** (an ootheca, which in mantises starts as a froth and hardens), and **many tiny nymphs hatch out of it together**, each a small wingless copy of the adult (three stages: egg, nymph, adult). **How sure: very high (about 95%)**. "Cousin" is a child's word for "close relative". No number of babies is given (it varies by kind, from a few dozen to a few hundred).
