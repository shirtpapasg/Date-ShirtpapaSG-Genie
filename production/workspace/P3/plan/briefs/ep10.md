# EP 10 — A metal ship floats but a metal spoon sinks. Why?

**Topic tag:** Float, Sink, Waterproof · **Icon:** 🚢 · **Chapter:** Ch 3 pp. 56–57 · **Mind Lab chapter:** Diversity of Materials · **School Lab:** 3.5 The drip test · Part (a): Drip on sheet one (there is no lab for 3.4 float or sink) · **NGSS:** 2-PS1-2 · **Narrator:** Heart voice 3 · **Target:** 3:30–4:00, about 480 authored words · **Output:** `FloatSinkWaterproof`

## Big idea
The material of an object affects whether it floats or sinks (a metal ship floats because of trapped air); materials that do not absorb water at all are waterproof, and a treatment such as wax can make fabric waterproof.

## Misconceptions to bust
- "Heavy things always sink and light things always float." A huge ship floats; the material and trapped air matter (app M-33).
- "Metal always sinks." Most metal objects do; a ship (or a bowl) floats because of trapped air; fill the bowl and it sinks.
- "Waterproof means it dries fast." It means it does not absorb water at all.
- "Fabric can never be waterproof." Wax makes it waterproof.

## Exam wording box
1. The material of an object affects the ability of the object to float or sink. Most metals sink in water. Most types of wood float on water.
2. There is a lot of trapped air inside the ship. The trapped air helps the ship to float on water.
3. Materials that do not absorb water at all are waterproof.
