## Big idea
A magnet has two poles (North and South) where attraction is strongest; unlike poles attract and like poles repel; repulsion is the test that proves an object is a magnet.

## Misconceptions to bust
- "A magnet is strongest in the middle." Strongest at the poles.
- "Only bar magnets have poles / repel." Every magnet has two poles, whatever the shape.
- "Anything attracted to a magnet is a magnet." Only repulsion proves it (monograph C4).
- "Pushing apart means North and North." Like poles: North and North, or South and South.
- "A floating ring magnet is broken." Like poles are repelling.
- Say "unlike" and "like" (the book's words), not "opposite" and "same"; say "attract" and "repel", not "stick".

## Realism notes
- Poles: N red, S blue, white letters; every drawn magnet has both.
- Clips cluster at the two ends of the bar magnet; the middle is bare.
- Pull: two arrows pointing toward each other between N and S; push: two arrows pointing away between N and N (motion arrows, allowed); the gap between like poles never closes.
- Ring magnets on a vertical rod: the floating ring has a visible gap; its lower face is labelled N above an N face.
- The home try uses bar magnets or round whiteboard magnets (flat fridge magnets are striped sheets and do not show like poles repelling); "Ask an adult" badge.
- The ✗ sits at the start of the wrong statement; no strikeout.
