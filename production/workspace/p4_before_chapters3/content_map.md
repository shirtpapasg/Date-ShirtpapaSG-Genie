## THEME OPENER: SYSTEMS (pp. 2–3)

- **Hook:** a bicycle. Students ask which parts must work together to make it move (pedals, chain, seat, spokes, tyres, bell, handle). They then decide whether the bike still works if the chain falls off, the spokes are damaged or a pedal is missing (a tick-box activity).
- **Big idea:**
  - A system is made of parts, and each part has its own special job.
  - The parts interact to perform a function.
  - If one part fails, the whole system is likely to be affected.
- **Key inquiry questions (paraphrased):**
  - S1: What is a system?
  - S2: How do parts or systems work together to do a job?
  - S3: Why does it matter to understand how they work together?
- Students are prompted to name other everyday systems and their parts.

## CHAPTER 1: PLANT SYSTEM (Theme: Systems, pp. 4–21)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
- Name the parts of a plant (leaves, stem, roots) and state the function of each.
- Recognise that the same parts look different in different plants, and that the differences help each plant survive in its environment. Observe similarities and differences.
- The opener links back to the bicycle: plant parts work together so that the plant grows well.

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Three main parts:** leaves, stem and roots. Each part has a different job, and together they help the plant grow well. (p. 5 diagram: plant labelled roots, stem, leaf.)
2. **Leaves: making food.** Many leaves are green because they contain a green substance that absorbs light, which the plant uses to make food.
3. **Leaves: gas exchange.** Leaves usually have tiny openings that let gases move into and out of the leaf.
4. **Parts of a leaf:**
   - **Leaf blade:** usually the biggest part. Its edge sets the leaf's shape.
   - **Leaf stalk:** attaches the leaf to the stem. It can be long or short.
   - **Main vein:** joined to the leaf stalk.
   - **Smaller veins:** branch off the main vein.
5. **Vein patterns:** veins can be **parallel** or arranged as a **net**.
6. **Stem: support.** The stem holds the plant upright and supports the leaves so they get enough light.
7. **Stem: transport.** The stem carries water, mineral salts and food to different parts of the plant.
8. **Types of stem:**
   - **Woody** stems are hard. Trees have thick woody stems and shrubs have thin woody stems.
   - **Non-woody** stems are soft, as in many herbs (rosemary, oregano, parsley, mint).
   - Stems can be adapted. For example, the baobab's stem stores water so the tree survives dry conditions.
9. **Roots:**
   - Some roots anchor the plant firmly in the ground.
   - Roots absorb water and mineral salts, which the stem then carries around the plant. Without roots, a plant cannot take in water and mineral salts and soon dies.
10. **Storage roots:** some roots store food made by the leaves, for use later (carrot, turnip, sweet potato).
11. **Stem cuttings:** a piece of stem placed in water grows new roots and leaves. Planted in soil, it becomes a new plant. This leads into a prompt about other ways plants reproduce.
12. **Diversity of plant parts:** the parts look different from plant to plant but do similar jobs. The differences help each plant survive where it grows.
    - **Leaves** vary in edge (straight or jagged), shape (oval or round), colour (mostly green, but also purple or red) and texture (waxy or hairy).
    - **Cactus:**
      - Its leaves are spines, which reduce water loss and defend against animals.
      - Its green stem makes the food instead of the leaves.
    - **Weak stems:** some plants have weak, non-woody stems.
      - Climbers coil around structures to reach more light.
      - Creepers spread along the ground and fan out their leaves to catch light.
    - **Roots:**
      - Water plants such as water lettuce have hairy-looking roots that hang in water.
      - Big trees such as the Rain Tree have strong, sturdy roots in the soil.

### Activities / investigations
- **AB 1.1 / SLS "Plant Parts":** identify plant parts.
- **AB 1.2 / SLS "Functions of Plant Parts":** match each part to its functions.
- **AB 1.3:** compare roots of different plants.
- **AB 1.4:** a wrap-up about the diversity of plants.
- **Stem-cutting demo (photo sequence):** cutting in water → roots and leaves appear after a few days → more growth after a few more days.
- **Let's Think and Do, "Posing questions":** the four students explore the school garden and turn what they notice into questions. Examples:
  - "Is this a plant if it has no leaves like the others?" (a cactus)
  - "Why does this plant coil around a pole?"
  - This models how scientists start from observations.
- **Let's Apply Q2 (data-based):**
  - **Set-up:** two similar plants, with most leaves removed from plant B. Both are kept in the same place and watered the same.
  - **Measured:** height at week 0 and week 2, shown as a bar graph.
  - **Skill:** read the graph step by step (A's change, B's change, compare at week 0, compare at week 2).
  - **Inference:** fewer leaves means less food is made, so the plant grows less.

### Real-world / Singapore contexts
- **I'm Inspired:** Henry Nicholas Ridley, first director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens. Through perseverance he found a way to tap latex from rubber trees without killing them, which helped build the region's rubber industry. The page also explains what botanists do (conservation, agriculture).
- **Leafy vegetables** such as kai lan, nai bai and spinach, where we eat mainly the leaves.
- **Firewood** for winter warmth and **log cabins** as uses of woody stems.
- **Sweet potato:** easy to grow, keeps longer than leaves, rich in vitamins and minerals, and can replace rice during food shortages.
- **Singapore's "30 by 30" goal:** produce 30% of the nation's food needs by 2030. Community edible gardens support it, and it ties to the "City in Nature" vision, greening Singapore and community bonding.
- Plants attract butterflies and bees.

### Misconceptions / exam points
- **Function wording** expected in answers:
  - Leaves "make food" and "take in and give out gases".
  - Stem "holds the plant upright so leaves get light" and "transports water, mineral salts and food".
  - Roots "hold the plant firmly to the ground" and "absorb water and mineral salts".
- Roots absorb **water and mineral salts**. They do not absorb food.
- The stem transports **both** water and mineral salts (up from the roots) and food (from the leaves).
- In a cactus the **stem**, not the leaves, makes food. Spines are modified leaves.
- **Let's Apply Q1:** identify a labelled part (part X) and choose its function from four options.
- **Let's Apply Q3:** infer from a plant climbing a pole that its stem is weak and non-woody, and explain that climbing gets more light for food-making.
- **Graph reading:** compare changes over time, not just the final values.

## CHAPTER 2: HUMAN SYSTEMS (Theme: Systems, pp. 22–41)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
- Name the systems of the human body and their functions.
- Name the parts of the digestive system and their functions.
- Explain how the parts of the digestive system work together.
- **Opener:** after running, Ali's heart beats faster, he breathes faster and he feels hungry. Which systems are responsible?

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. The body is made of many systems working together in harmony. The five covered are respiratory, circulatory, skeletal, muscular and digestive.
2. **Models (Let's Think and Do: "Using models"):** scientists use models to represent body systems. Real bodies are more complex than the models, and students compare the models with the real body.
3. **Respiratory system:** the exchange of gases between the body and the surroundings. Parts: nose, windpipe, lungs.
4. **Circulatory system:**
   - Works closely with the respiratory system.
   - Carries digested food and oxygen to all parts of the body, and carries waste away for removal.
   - Parts: heart, and blood vessels carrying blood.
5. **Skeletal system:**
   - Made of bones. It gives the body its shape and protects important organs.
   - Parts: skull, rib cage, backbone, other bones.
6. **Muscular system:**
   - Works closely with the skeletal system to move body parts.
   - Some muscles are under our control (arm). Some are not (heart).
   - Bones cannot move on their own. Muscles work in pairs: one contracts while the other relaxes, as in moving the arm.
7. **Digestive system:**
   - Breaks food down into simpler substances (digestion) so the body can use it.
   - Parts: mouth, gullet, stomach, small intestine, large intestine.
8. **Mouth:**
   - Teeth chew food into smaller pieces. This makes swallowing easier and exposes more food surface to digestive juices, so digestion is faster.
   - Saliva softens food and contains digestive juices.
   - The tongue tastes food and rolls it into a ball for swallowing.
   - Digestion **begins** in the mouth.
9. **Gullet:** a muscular tube that pushes food from the mouth to the stomach. It lies next to the windpipe.
10. **Stomach:**
    - A muscular bag that holds food for a few hours.
    - It makes digestive juices and mixes them with the food, which partially digests it.
11. **Small intestine:**
    - Its walls produce more digestive juices.
    - Digestion **ends** here.
    - Digested food is **absorbed into the blood** and carried around the body.
12. **Large intestine:**
    - Receives undigested food and **absorbs water** from it.
    - The undigested remains are stored in the **rectum** and leave through the **anus**. (These are named in a fact box, not in the main list of parts.)
13. **Working together:**
    - All the parts must work well for food to be digested properly. If one fails, the person feels unwell.
    - Example: removing much of the small intestine reduces absorption, so the person may become malnourished.

### Key vocabulary
system, respiratory system, nose, windpipe, lungs, exchange of gases, circulatory system, heart, blood vessels, blood, oxygen, digested food, waste materials, skeletal system, bones, skull, rib cage, backbone, femur, muscular system, muscles, contract, relax, digestive system, digestion, digestive juices, saliva, teeth, tongue, mouth, gullet, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum, anus, absorbed, undigested food, simpler substances, model, malnourished, fibre.

- **AB 2.2 Part B: the chewing model.**

  - This is the analogy for chewing (surface area idea, in kid language).
- **AB 2.3 / SLS "Digestive System":** how the parts work together.
- **AB 2.4:** a further digestive-system activity.
- **Let's Explore prompts:**
  - Find out what happens with gastric pain.
  - Think about what toilet discomfort you would get from eating too little fibre (constipation is implied). Fibre from fruit and vegetables helps undigested food move through the large intestine.
- **Let's Think and Do:** compare model representations of the five systems with the real body.

### Real-world contexts
- Running and its effect on heartbeat, breathing and hunger.
- **Chen's toothache:** he cannot chew, so he struggles to swallow and eat. Have your teeth checked.
- **Safety:** don't talk while swallowing, because food may enter the windpipe instead of the gullet and cause choking and coughing.
- **Body facts:**
  - The femur (thigh bone) is the longest and strongest bone, so it often survives in skeletal remains.
  - The smallest bones are inside the ear.
  - An adult's small intestine is about 6.5 m long and coiled in the abdomen.
  - An adult's large intestine is about 1.5 m long, shorter than the small intestine but wider.
- Gastric pain, fibre and healthy eating.
- **I'm Inspired:** Leonardo da Vinci, artist and scientist. His curiosity-driven, accurate anatomy drawings show arts and sciences working together.

### Misconceptions / exam points
- Digestion **starts in the mouth** and **ends in the small intestine**. It does **not** end in the large intestine.
- The **large intestine absorbs water**. It does not digest food.
- **Digested food is absorbed into the blood in the small intestine.**
- Q4(b–c) style: there is more digested food in the small intestine than in the stomach, because more digestion happens in the small intestine.
- Undigested food leaves through the anus (rectum and anus are not "digestive parts" in the main list).
- The gullet only transports food. It does not digest it.
- Keep the two jobs of the teeth distinct: making swallowing easier, and exposing more surface to juices for faster digestion.
- The heart is a muscle we cannot control. Arm muscles can be controlled.
- The muscular and skeletal systems work together, and so do the respiratory and circulatory systems.
- Function wording to use:
  - Skeletal: "gives the body shape and protects important parts".
  - Circulatory: "carries digested food and oxygen to body parts, and removes waste".
- **Let's Apply:** Q1 identifies a system from its parts. Q2 matches systems to function statements.

## CHAPTER 3: MATTER (Theme: Cycles, pp. 42–79)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
- What is matter?
- What are the three states of matter, and what are their properties?
- How are mass and volume measured for solids, liquids and gases?
- Skills: using apparatus to gather data, and designing and conducting a fair-test investigation (hypothesis, variables).

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Scientific meaning of "matter":**
   - In everyday speech, "What's the matter?" means asking about a problem.
   - In science, matter is **anything that has mass and occupies space** (cups, books, water, air).
2. **Mass:**
   - Mass is the amount of matter in an object. More matter means greater mass, and heavier things take more effort to carry.
   - Examples: two water bottles, one fuller than the other; a balance tilts toward 10 bricks rather than 2.
   - The electronic balance gives a larger reading for the fuller bottle (example values 1000 g compared with 250 g; 20 kg compared with 4 kg for the bricks).
3. **Occupying space:**
   - A full bottle can't take more water. A bag full of books can't fit a lunch box.
   - You must remove some matter to make room (Devi's lunch box and water bottle scenes).
4. **Volume:**
   - Volume is the amount of space an object occupies. Two cubes have a greater volume than one cube.
   - It is measured with a measuring cylinder or beaker in the lab, or a measuring cup at home.
5. **Non-matter:**
   - Non-matter has no mass and does not occupy space. Examples: **light, shadow, heat, sound**.
   - **Light:** passes through pond water. Shining it on a balance pan doesn't tilt the balance or give a reading.
   - **Shadow:** a person can stand inside a blind's shadow. It doesn't tilt a balance.
   - **Heat:** the Sun's heat passes through walls and glass, and gives no reading on a balance.
   - **Sound:** passes through walls and has no mass.
6. **Air is matter** even though it is invisible.
   - **Has mass:** a balance tilts toward an inflated basketball rather than a partly deflated one.
   - **Occupies space:**
     - Blowing through a straw into an inverted cup in water makes bubbles, and the water level inside the cup drops because air takes up that space.
     - Another version shows an inverted cup pushed into water.
7. **Classifying matter by physical properties** (colour, shape, volume). The key properties are whether the shape and the volume are definite.
8. **Solid: definite shape and definite volume.**
   - Examples: a hammer keeps its shape on a table or in a toolbox, and won't squeeze into a full toolbox (it can't be compressed). Bricks keep their shape when stacked, and coins in a jar.
   - Solids cannot be compressed and do not fill all the space they are given.
9. **Liquid: no definite shape, but definite volume.**
   - **Tea:** takes the shape of the teapot, then of the jug. It overflows a full small cup because it can't be compressed, and doesn't fill a large teapot.
   - **Juice:** takes the carton's shape and won't squeeze into a small glass.
   - **Oil:** takes the ladle's shape, then spreads in the pan without filling it.
   - **Water shooter toy:** water squirts out because it cannot be compressed.
10. **Gas: no definite shape and no definite volume.**
    - **Air:**
      - Takes a cylindrical pump's shape, then a basketball's spherical shape.
      - More air can still be pumped into an already inflated ball, because air can be compressed.
      - A gas fills all the available space.
    - **Syringe:** pull the plunger and the air fills the space; cover the nozzle and push, and the air is compressed into less space.
    - **Uses of compression:**
      - Gases are transported compressed in cylinders.
      - A scuba diver's air tank holds compressed air, and with no fixed shape the air flows through tubes to the diver.
      - Helium cylinders fill balloons of different shapes, and helium balloons float.
11. **Earth has all three states:** solid rock, liquid water covering most of the surface, and a layer of gaseous air.
12. **Measuring mass:**
    - Units are kilograms (kg) and grams (g), for example a 5 kg bag of rice.
    - The simple balance was used historically: the object goes on one pan and known masses on the other until level. The electronic balance is faster and more accurate.
13. **Using an electronic balance correctly:**
    1. Place it on a **flat, stable surface** indoors with **no wind**. An unsteady surface or wind gives inaccurate readings.
    2. Press "On" **before** putting anything on the pan.
    3. The reading must be **zero** before weighing. If it isn't (a "zero error", for example 0.5 g), press **"Tare"/"Zero"**.
14. **Mass of a solid:** place it directly on the zeroed balance and read the value.
15. **Mass of a liquid:**
    - A liquid needs a container, so weigh the empty container, then the container with the liquid, and subtract.
    - Example: beaker 64.1 g, beaker with water 138.0 g, so the water is 73.9 g.
16. **Mass of a gas:**
    - Same subtraction idea.
    - Example: deflated basketball 586 g, inflated 597 g, so the air is 11 g.
17. **Measuring volume:**
    - Measuring cylinders and beakers are both used. The cylinder is **more accurate** because its markings are finer.
    - Units are cm³ and ml, where **1 cm³ = 1 ml**.
18. **Volume of a liquid:**
    - Put the cylinder on a flat surface.
    - The water surface is curved. Read from the **bottom of the curved surface** with your **eye level with it** (example reading: 90 cm³).
19. **Volume of a solid (displacement):**
    - Record the water volume, lower a stone tied on a string gently in, and record the new volume.
    - The difference is the stone's volume (60 − 50 = 10 cm³).
20. **Volume of a gas:**
    - Fill a measuring cylinder completely with water and invert it, clamped, in a basin of water.
    - Blow air in through a straw. The gas pushes the water out, and the reading equals the volume of gas.
21. **Designing and conducting investigations (Let's Think and Do):**
    - **Scenario:** a plasticine tower topples. One student suggests flattening the heavy piece to make it lighter. The question becomes: does changing the shape change the mass?
    - **Hypothesis:** not a random guess, but a prediction based on known information. Mass is the amount of matter, and reshaping doesn't change the amount of matter, so shape should not affect mass.
    - **Fair test:** change only one variable and keep the others the same. Shown as a variables table:
      - Changed: the shape of the plasticine.
      - Kept the same: the volume and type of plasticine.
      - Measured: the mass (on an electronic balance).
    - **Method:** weigh the rectangular block; flatten it with the palm, removing none, and weigh; roll it into a ball and weigh; roll it into a log and weigh. Record the shape and mass each time.
    - **Results:** all 20.0 g, so the hypothesis is supported and the conclusion is that shape does not affect mass.
    - **If results don't support a hypothesis:** that's okay. Revisit the hypothesis, gather more information, or rethink the design. The student realises the "flatten to make it lighter" idea was wrong.

### Misconceptions / exam points
- **Air is matter.** It is invisible but has mass and occupies space. Common trap: students say air is "nothing".
- **Light, shadow, heat and sound are NOT matter.** No mass, no space.
- **Shape does not change mass.** Flattening plasticine does not make it lighter.
- Cutting a solid into pieces does not change its total volume, so the water level stays the same.
- A gas has no definite volume: it fills its container entirely. Air volume equals container volume minus the solid's volume.
- Same size does not mean same mass (cube X compared with cube Y).
- **Required property wording:**
  - Solid: definite shape **and** definite volume.
  - Liquid: no definite shape **but** definite volume.
  - Gas: no definite shape **and** no definite volume.
  - Solids and liquids cannot be compressed. Gases can.
- Measuring rules:
  - Zero or tare the balance first.
  - A liquid's or gas's mass must be found by **subtracting** the container's mass.
  - Read the measuring cylinder at eye level from the bottom of the curved surface, on a flat surface.
  - A measuring cylinder is more accurate than a beaker.
- 1 cm³ = 1 ml.
- **Investigation vocabulary:**
  - Fair test: only one variable is changed.
  - The hypothesis is based on known information, not a guess.
  - State whether the results support the hypothesis.

## CHAPTER 4: LIGHT (Theme: Energy, pp. 82–93)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
- Where does light come from (natural and man-made sources)?
- How does light help us see? Either it enters the eye directly from a source, or it is reflected off objects into the eye.

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Opener:** Belle asks Mum to leave a light on at bedtime, and Mum gives her a dim night light. Without light we can't see, can't find things, and may trip or bump into things. Light helps us find things and avoid accidents.
2. **Sources of light** are objects that give off light. They can be natural or man-made.
3. **Natural sources:**
   - **The Sun:** a star and Earth's main light source, lighting things during the day.
   - **Other stars:** they give off light, but too little reaches Earth to light up our surroundings at night.
   - **Some living things:** some fireflies, jellyfish, deep-sea plants and glow-worms produce light, so they can be seen at night or in the dark deep sea.
   - **Natural happenings:** lightning during thunderstorms.
4. **Man-made sources:**
   - Light bulbs (a table lamp) and fireworks.
   - They light our surroundings when natural light is absent. Fireworks also make celebration displays.
5. **Seeing:** for us to see, **light must enter our eyes**. Blindfold tag shows that Chen can't see even in bright sun.
6. **Seeing light sources:** their light enters our eyes **directly** (stars, candle flame, fireflies, a light bulb).
7. **Seeing non-luminous objects:**
   - Most things don't give off light, and a light source must be present.
   - Light from the source falls on the object and **bounces off (is reflected)**. When the reflected light enters the eye, we see the object.
   - **Worked two-step examples** (numbered arrows):
     - A torch lights a pencil, and light is reflected from the pencil into Belle's eyes (in a blackout).
     - A lamp at night lights the sofa, and light is reflected from the sofa into her eyes.
     - Sunlight lights a swing, and light is reflected from it into her eyes.
     - In the daytime, sunlight comes through the window onto the sofa and is reflected into her eyes, so no lamp is needed.
8. **The Moon is not a light source.** We see it because sunlight falls on it and is reflected to our eyes (diagram of Sun → Moon → Earth).

### Key vocabulary
light, source of light, natural source of light, man-made source of light, the Sun (a star), stars, lightning, fireflies, glow-worms, jellyfish, deep-sea plants, light bulb, fireworks, reflect / reflected light, enters our eyes, blackout, torch.

### Activities / investigations
- **AB 4.1:** how we see. Let's Inquire includes a blindfold game.
- **SLS "Light".**
- No formal variable-based experiment in this chapter. It is mainly classification and ray-path explanation.

### Diagrams / visuals
- Photos of the Sun, stars, glowing living things, lightning, fireflies' light patterns, a table lamp and fireworks.
- Four "we see it directly" photos: stars, a candle flame, fireflies, a light bulb.
- **Two-arrow ray diagrams:** source → object (step 1), then object → eye (step 2), for the pencil and torch, sofa and lamp, swing and Sun, and sofa and window sunlight.
- Moon-Earth-Sun reflection diagram.

### Real-world contexts
- Night lights at bedtime. A blackout at home (getting a torch from the kitchen drawer). Playing in a playground.
- Fireflies use light patterns to recognise one another. Fireworks at celebrations.
- **I'm Inspired:** Thomas Edison made the first light bulb practical for homes, because earlier bulbs were too big or too bright. The page highlights his persistence and resilience.

### Misconceptions / exam points
- **The Moon is NOT a source of light.** It reflects sunlight.
- We do not see because "the eyes send out light" or merely because "it is bright". **Light must enter the eyes.**
- For non-luminous objects, both steps are needed: light from a source falls on the object, **and** it is reflected into the eyes.
- Answer wording: name the source, say the light falls on the object, and say it is reflected into the eyes.
- Stars other than the Sun are sources too, but their light is too dim to light up our surroundings.
- **Living sources** (fireflies, etc.) are natural sources.

## CHAPTER 5: SHADOWS (Theme: Energy, pp. 94–119)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
- What are shadows?
- Which properties of light explain shadow formation?
- How do shadows form?
- What affects the size and shape of shadows?

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Opener:** Grandpa explains **wayang kulit**, traditional shadow-puppet theatre on a cloth screen, watched before TV existed.
2. **Recap from Primary 3 (Diversity of Materials):**
   - **Opaque** objects let no light through.
   - **Translucent** objects let some light through.
   - **Transparent** objects let most light through.
3. **Shadow (definition):**
   - The dark area formed on a surface when an opaque or translucent object blocks light from falling on that surface.
   - Cartoon: Chen blocks the projector light.
   - Understanding shadows lets us make shadow puppets.
4. **Two properties of light explain shadows:**
   - (a) **Light travels in straight lines.**
   - (b) **Light cannot pass through opaque objects. Only some light passes through translucent ones.**
5. **Evidence that light travels in straight lines:**
   - Laser light shows.
   - The **three-card experiment:** see Activities below.
   - Let's Explore asks for other ways to show it.
   - **Safety warning:** never look directly at the Sun or other bright sources.
6. **Opaque objects:**
   - Light travels straight until it hits an object. A wooden board blocks all light, giving a dark area on the wall.
   - **Translucent objects** (frosted glass): some light passes through, giving a **less dark** area.
7. **How shadows form:** when light is completely or partly blocked.
   - Lamp-and-tabletop demo:
     - An opaque cardboard gives a dark shadow.
     - A frosted plastic sheet gives a less dark shadow.
     - Areas not blocked stay bright.
8. **Size of shadow** depends on:
   - (i) the distance between the object and the light source;
   - (ii) the distance between the object and the screen. The surface a shadow falls on is called the **screen**.
   - **Light source moved further from the object** → smaller shadow. **Closer** → bigger shadow (torch, ball, screen).
   - **Screen moved further from the object** → bigger shadow. **Closer** → smaller shadow.
   - **Moving the object** between a fixed torch and screen changes both distances together:
     - Ball nearer the torch (so further from the screen) → bigger shadow.
     - Ball nearer the screen → smaller shadow.
9. **Modelling with light rays (Let's Think and Do: "Using and developing models"):**
   - Draw straight rays from the torch past the top and bottom edges of the ball to the screen. The gap between where they land predicts the shadow size.
   - The diagram shows positions A, B and C of the ball, with the shadow shrinking as the ball nears the screen.
10. **Outdoor shadows (Science Around Us):**
    - Our bodies block sunlight. Shadows are long early morning and late afternoon, when the Sun is low, and shortest near noon, when the Sun is overhead.
    - Morning and evening shadows point opposite ways, because the Sun rises in the East and sets in the West.
    - Ancient people used **sundials** to tell time. Students are asked what the disadvantage would be (implied: no use at night or on cloudy days).
    - A tree diagram at 8 a.m., 12 noon and 4 p.m. opens the size section.
11. **Shape of shadow:**
    - The object's shape sets the shadow's shape: a round object gives a round shadow.
    - The same object can cast different shadows depending on **which part of it the light shines on**. A **cone** gives:
      - a triangle when lit from the side;
      - a circle when lit at its base, or even with the tip facing the light, because the circular base still blocks the light.
12. **Hand shadow puppets:** Belle's bird-shaped hands don't make a bird shadow. Ali advises turning her hands so the shape she wants faces the light.
13. **Shadow art:** artists arrange objects and position light sources to create surprising shadow images.

### Key vocabulary
shadow, opaque, translucent, transparent, block (completely / partially), light travels in straight lines, screen, source of light, light rays, distance between object and light source, distance between object and screen, size of shadow, shape of shadow, sundial, wayang kulit, shadow puppet, laser.

### Activities / investigations
- **AB 5.1 Part A / SLS "Light Travels in Straight Lines": the three-card experiment.**
  - Cards A, B and C have holes and stand on bases in front of a lit candle.
  - When the holes are lined up, Ali sees the flame. When card B is shifted, the light is blocked and he sees nothing.
  - Conclusion: light travels in straight lines.
- **AB 5.1 Part B:** light through opaque compared with translucent materials, giving a dark compared with a less dark area.
- **SLS "Shadows":** opaque cardboard compared with frosted plastic over a tabletop under a lamp.
- **AB 5.2: shadow size investigations.**
  - Changed: the torch-to-ball distance, or the ball-to-screen distance, or the ball's position.
  - Measured: shadow size.
  - Expected:
    - Light nearer → bigger shadow.
    - Screen further → bigger shadow.
    - Ball nearer the torch → bigger shadow.
- **AB 5.3 / SLS "Positions and Lengths of Shadows":** outdoor shadow length and position through the day (8 a.m., 10 a.m., 12 noon, 2 p.m., 4 p.m.).
- **AB 5.4:** shadows of different shapes on a screen.
- **AB 5.5 / SLS "Shapes of Shadows":** the cone orientation and hand shadows.
- **SLS "Sizes of Shadows":** the ray-drawing model.

### Diagrams / visuals
- Wayang kulit photo.
- Laser light show.
- Three-card set-up, aligned compared with misaligned.
- Lamp, wooden board and wall (dark area). Lamp, frosted glass and wall (less dark area).
- Lamp over a tabletop with cardboard and frosted plastic, with labelled bright, dark and less dark zones.
- **Torch–ball–screen diagrams:** the torch moved, the screen moved, the ball moved.
- **Ray diagram:** ball at positions A, B and C with a size bracket on the screen for each.
- Tree shadows at 8 a.m., 12 noon and 4 p.m. A person's shadow through the day. A sundial photo.
- Shadows of various shapes. A cone lit from two orientations (triangle compared with circle). Hand shadow puppets. A shadow-art sculpture.
- Exam visuals:
  - **Q1:** cards A, B and C with a light source and eye. Diagram 2 has card A out of line. Asks whether the light can be seen, why, and which property of light this shows.
  - **Q2:** wooden pieces A and B (4 cm each, different shapes) at different distances from the light. Choose the correct shadows: the nearer piece casts a larger shadow, and each shadow's shape depends on the piece.
  - **Q3:** two identical solids in different orientations under two lights. Choose the shadows on screens A and B.
  - **Q4:** Belle walks A → C → B past a street lamp. Choose the graph of shadow length against time (the shadow shortens near the lamp and lengthens away from it).
- **End concept map** (pp. 118–119), combining light and shadow:
  - Light is a form of energy, with natural and man-made sources, and light rays drawn as arrows.
  - Its function is to help us see, either directly or by reflection.
  - Its properties (straight lines; opaque and translucent blocking) result in shadows.
  - Shadow size depends on the two distances. Shadow shape depends on the part lit.

### Real-world / Singapore contexts
- **Wayang kulit**, a regional traditional shadow puppetry. Grandpa's childhood entertainment.
- Laser light shows. A projector blocked by a person.
- Our shadows on sunny days. Sundials. Shadow art.
- Sun direction: rises in the East, sets in the West.

### Misconceptions / exam points
- **Translucent objects also form shadows.** The shadow is less dark, not absent.
- **Transparent** objects let most light through and form at most a very faint shadow (implied).
- A shadow is not "the object's reflection" and is not matter.
- **Definition wording:** a shadow is a dark area formed on a surface when an opaque or translucent object blocks light.
- **Two properties** must both be cited to explain a shadow: light travels in straight lines, **and** light cannot pass through an opaque object (only some passes through a translucent one).
- **Size rules** (state them precisely; students often mix them up):
  - Object closer to the light → bigger shadow.
  - Object further from the screen → bigger shadow.
  - When the object moves, both distances change.
- **Shape** depends on the **part of the object facing the light**, not simply the object's 3D shape. A cone can cast a circle even with its tip toward the light.
- **Outdoor shadow length:** longest when the Sun is low (morning or late afternoon), shortest at noon. Morning and evening shadows point in opposite directions.
- Walking past a lamp: the shadow shortens as you approach and lengthens as you walk away (interpreting a graph).
- Safety: never stare at bright sources.

## CHAPTER 6: HEAT (Theme: Energy, pp. 120–133)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
- What is temperature, and how is it measured?
- What causes temperature to change?
- How do heat and temperature differ?

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Opener:**
   - Cold milk warmed in a basin of hot water, hot soup too hot to drink, feeling sweaty, a fan.
   - Drawing curtains to stop sunlight heating the room.
   - Idea: objects get hotter or colder because their temperature changes, and heat causes that change.
2. **Need for temperature:**
   - Doctors need your body temperature when you have a fever. Bakers need the right oven temperature.
   - Belle compares four cups of water with words (hotter, colder, hottest) and finds it confusing. Numbers are better.
3. **Temperature:**
   - A measure of how hot or cold something is, expressed as a number in **degrees Celsius (°C)**.
   - A bigger number means a higher temperature (cups at 50, 40, 28 and 10 °C).
4. **Thermal camera (Science Around Us):**
   - Uses colours to show temperature. Hot people appear red.
   - Used at airports and hospitals to screen for fever without taking each person's temperature.
5. **Touch is unreliable for exact values**, so we use a **thermometer**. Types:
   - **Digital thermometer:** body temperature.
   - **Infrared thermometer:** surface temperature of solids and liquids.
   - **Laboratory thermometer:**
     - For liquids and gases.
     - **Cannot measure a solid directly**, because the bulb must be fully surrounded.
6. **Using a lab thermometer (Let's Think and Do: "Using apparatus and equipment to gather data"):**
   1. Fully immerse the bulb in the liquid.
   2. Wait until the red liquid stops rising or falling.
   3. Read at eye level from the bottom of the curved surface of the red liquid.
7. **Data logger:** a digital device that shows temperature when connected to a temperature sensor. Useful in investigations.
8. **What changes temperature:**
   - Cold milk in hot water: the milk warms and the water cools.
   - Explanation: **heat**. Like light, heat is a **form of energy**.
   - **Gain heat → temperature rises. Lose heat → temperature falls.**
   - The milk gains the heat the hot water loses. **Heat flows from the hot water to the cold milk.**
   - Example values: milk 10 → 30 °C, water 60 → 45 °C, with arrows showing the direction of heat flow.
9. **Temperature compared with heat:**
   - Temperature is a **property** of an object, a measure of hotness. Heat is a **form of energy** that **causes** temperature to change.
   - In short: temperature is what we measure, and heat is why it changes.
   - Dialogue: Ali says "I have a lot of heat" after a run. Mr Bala corrects him that he means his temperature is higher.
10. **Cooling and warming curves:**
    - **Hot water left on a table:** it loses heat to its surroundings, so its temperature falls over time toward room temperature (graph).
    - **Cold water on a table:** it gains heat from its surroundings, so its temperature rises toward room temperature (graph with a "room temperature" level marked).

### Key vocabulary
temperature, degree Celsius (°C), hot / cold, thermometer (digital, infrared, laboratory), bulb, data logger, temperature sensor, thermal camera, heat, form of energy, gain heat, lose heat, heat flow, surroundings, room temperature, property.

### Activities / investigations
- **AB 6.1 / SLS "Temperature":** compare and measure the temperatures of cups of water. A hot-objects safety warning applies.
- **AB 6.2 / SLS "Heat Flow":** cold milk bottle (or similar) in hot water. Measure both temperatures over time. Expected: they converge.
- **AB 6.3 / SLS "Difference Between Heat and Temperature":**
  - Beakers of hot and cold water left on a table, temperature measured over time.
  - Expected: hot water falls and cold water rises, both toward room temperature.

### Diagrams / visuals
- Four cups with thermometers (later labelled 50, 40, 28 and 10 °C).
- Thermal camera image.
- Photos of digital, infrared and laboratory thermometers.
- Step diagrams for the lab thermometer, including eye level at the bottom of the curved surface.
- Data logger and sensor photos.
- Milk bottle in a basin with heat-flow arrows and temperature labels.
- Comparison table: temperature compared with heat.
- Line graphs of temperature against time: hot water decreasing, and cold water increasing, each levelling toward a room-temperature line. Beaker sketches with "heat lost" and "heat gained" arrows.

### Real-world contexts
- Fever checks, baking, warming milk, soup cooling, curtains blocking sunlight.
- Thermal fever screening at airports and hospitals (a familiar sight in Singapore, especially post-COVID).

### Misconceptions / exam points
- **Heat ≠ temperature.** Never say a person "has more heat" to mean they are hot. Say their temperature is higher, or that they gained heat.
- Temperature is measured with a thermometer, in °C. Heat is the energy transferred.
- An object's temperature changes **because** it gains or loses heat. Answers should name the direction: what gained heat and from where, or what lost heat and to what.
- "Coldness" does not flow. Heat flows from the hotter to the colder object. This is stated explicitly in Chapter 7, and the milk example sets it up.
- Lab thermometer rules:
  - Keep the bulb fully immersed.
  - Wait for the reading to stabilise.
  - Read at eye level from the bottom of the curve.
  - Do not use it directly on solids.
- The **sense of touch is not accurate** for measuring temperature.

## CHAPTER 7: EFFECTS OF HEAT (Theme: Energy, pp. 134–159)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
- How does heat flow?
- What is a source of heat?
- How fast does heat flow through different materials (good and poor conductors)?
- What are the effects of gaining or losing heat (temperature change, expansion and contraction, change of state)?

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Opener scenarios:**
   - Ice cubes cool hot water.
   - A metal spoon in a hot drink gets hotter than a wooden one.
   - A cooler bag keeps canned drinks cold on a hot day.
   - Hot water loosens a tight jam-jar lid.
2. **Direction of heat flow:** heat **always** flows from an object at a higher temperature to one at a lower temperature. Worked observation-and-explanation tables:
   - **Pot of water on a stove:** the flame is hotter, so heat flows to the metal pot and water, and the water's temperature rises.
   - **Cold metal spoon in hot coffee:** heat flows from the coffee to the spoon, and the spoon feels hot.
   - **Hot tea on a table:** heat flows from the tea to its surroundings, so the tea cools while the table and air warm slightly.
3. **Line graphs (Let's Think and Do: "Analysing and interpreting data"):**
   - The data table (time 0, 5, 10, 15 min against temperature 100, 80, 68, 55 °C) is converted into a line graph.
   - Terms taught:
     - **Data point**, for example 80 °C at 5 min.
     - **Vertical axis** = the variable measured (temperature).
     - **Horizontal axis** = the variable changed (time).
     - A line joins the points to show the trend.
4. **Data logger experiment:**
   - A metal cup of water at 50 °C sits inside a plastic box of water at 20 °C, with sensors in both.
   - **Area A of the graph:** the temperatures differ, so heat flows from hot to cold. The 50 °C water falls and the 20 °C water rises.
   - **Area B:** after about 15 minutes both reach the same temperature (35 °C). Heat flow stops, and the temperatures stay constant.
   - Conclusion: heat flows only when there is a temperature difference, and continues **until both objects are at the same temperature**.
5. **Heat flow within one object:**
   - A metal rod with one end in a fire: that end gets hottest, and heat flows along the rod to the cooler parts.
   - So you shouldn't touch the far end, even though it isn't in the fire.
   - Summary: heat flows between objects **or areas** from higher to lower temperature until they are equal.
6. **Source of heat:**
   - An object that has a **higher temperature than its surroundings**.
   - Examples: the flame (pot on stove), the hot coffee (spoon), the hot tea (on the table).
   - **Natural sources:**
     - **The Sun** is Earth's major source of heat. The amount received varies with time of day and month.
     - **The Earth:** it is very hot beneath the surface, and this heats underground water, used for hot springs and cooking eggs in hot-spring water (the photo credits indicate a hot spring in Singapore).
7. **Rate of heat flow depends on material:**
   - A metal spoon heats faster than a wooden one in the same hot water.
   - Hot water stays hot longer in a polystyrene cup than in a metal cup.
8. **Wax-rod experiment:** see Activities below.
9. **Conductors:**
   - **Good conductors of heat** let heat flow through quickly. **Metals** are the example.
   - **Poor conductors of heat** let heat flow through slowly: **glass, plastic, wood**.
10. **Uses of conductors:**
    - **Good conductors:** metal pots, pans and kettles (heat reaches food or water quickly); the metal base of an electric iron (heat passes quickly to the fabric).
    - **Problem:** all-metal handles would be too hot to hold, so handles are made of **poor conductors** (plastic or wood).
    - A labelled photo set shows the metal body as a good conductor and the handle as a poor conductor on a pot, kettle, pan and iron.
11. **Air is a poor conductor of heat:**
    - A **wool** sweater traps more air between its fibres than cotton, so it slows heat loss from the body and keeps you warmer.
    - **Polystyrene** contains lots of trapped air, so it is a poor conductor. It is used for cooler boxes, keeping drinks and fresh fish cold longer.
12. **Effects of heat gain or loss:**
    - (a) temperature change;
    - (b) **expansion and contraction**;
    - (c) **change of state**.
13. **Expansion and contraction:**
    - **Gaining heat → the object expands** (its volume increases). **Losing heat → it contracts** (its volume decreases).
    - **Solid:** the ball-and-ring experiment (see Activities).
    - **Liquid:** a test tube of coloured water with a glass tube, placed in hot water. The coloured water level rises as it expands, and falls back when the heat source is removed as it loses heat and contracts.
    - **Gas:** a flask of air with a balloon over its mouth on a tray of hot water. The air expands and the balloon inflates. Remove the heat and the air contracts, so the balloon deflates.
14. **Applications:**
    - A tight metal jar lid placed in hot water expands, making it easier to open.
    - **Problems:** repeated expansion and contraction can crack roads that have no room to expand. Engineers add **expansion gaps / joints** in roads.
15. **Change of state:**
    - Ice gains heat and melts into water. Water heated on a stove gains heat and turns into steam.
    - Steam loses heat and turns back into water. Water in a freezer loses heat and turns into ice.
    - Cycle diagram: ice ⇄ water ⇄ steam, with "heat gained" one way and "heat lost" the other.
    - **Note:** the terms melting, boiling, evaporation, condensation and freezing are mostly not formally introduced here; only "melt" appears. These are left for the Cycles theme.

### Key vocabulary
heat flow, higher / lower temperature, source of heat, surroundings, natural source of heat (Sun, Earth), hot spring, data point, vertical axis, horizontal axis, line graph, data logger, temperature sensor, good conductor of heat, poor conductor of heat, metal, glass, plastic, wood, polystyrene, wool, cotton, trapped air, expand / expansion, contract / contraction, volume, change in the state of matter, melt, ice, water, steam, gain heat, lose heat, expansion gap / joint.

### Activities / investigations
- **AB 7.1 Part A / SLS "Heat Flow":** observe and explain heat-flow cases (stove, spoon, tea).
- **AB 7.1 Part B / SLS "Heat":** identify the source of heat in situations.
- **Data-logger mixing experiment** (described in item 4): hot metal cup inside a cool plastic box; temperatures converge to 35 °C.
- **AB 7.2 / SLS "Good and Poor Conductors of Heat": the wax-rod experiment.**
  - Metal, glass, plastic and wood rods, each with solid wax at the end, stand in the same container of hot water.
  - **Changed:** the rod material. **Measured:** how much wax melts in the same time.
  - **Result:** metal (most wax melted) > glass > plastic > wood (least).
  - **Inference:** heat flows fastest through metal and slowest through wood.
- **SLS "Uses of Good and Poor Conductors of Heat"** and **"Handling Hot Objects Safely".**
- **AB 7.3 / SLS "Expansion and Contraction"** (each set-up carries a safety warning for hot objects):
  - **Ball and ring (solid):**
    - The metal ball passes through the ring when cool.
    - After heating in a flame it no longer fits, because it expanded.
    - After cooling in cold water it fits again, because it contracted.
  - **Coloured water in a test tube with a glass tube (liquid):** the level rises in hot water and drops back on cooling.
  - **Flask and balloon (gas):** the balloon inflates on hot water and deflates on cooling.
- **AB 7.4:** changes of state with heat gain or loss.

    - A form of energy, different from temperature.
    - It flows from hotter to colder until equal.

### Misconceptions / exam points
- **Heat flows from hotter to colder. "Cold" does not flow.**
  - A spoon in cold water feels cold because the **hand loses heat to the spoon**. The spoon has lost heat to the cold water, so it is now colder than the hand.
- Heat flow **stops** when temperatures are equal. A graph that levels off means no further heat gain or loss.
- A **source of heat** is relative: anything hotter than its surroundings, such as hot tea.
- **Good and poor conductor wording:** good conductors allow heat to flow through them quickly; poor conductors do not.
- To keep something cold, use a poor conductor so it gains heat **slowly** from the surroundings. Use the same logic to keep something hot.
- Wool keeps you warm because it is a poor conductor (trapped air) that **slows heat loss from the body**. It does not "give heat".
- **Expansion and contraction wording:** gains heat → expands (volume increases); loses heat → contracts (volume decreases). Mass does not change (implied link to Chapter 3).
- Gases expand noticeably. Use the balloon and flask set-ups for reasoning.
- Changes of state need heat gain (ice → water → steam) or heat loss (steam → water → ice).
- Reading line graphs: the vertical axis is the measured variable and the horizontal axis is the changed variable. Identify trends and plateaus.
