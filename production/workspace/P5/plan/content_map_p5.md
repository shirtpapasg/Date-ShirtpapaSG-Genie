## CHAPTER 1: REPRODUCTION IN ANIMALS AND PLANTS (AB pp. 1–39)

**Key questions / outcomes.** Recognise that a cell is a basic unit of life. Describe the processes in the sexual reproduction of flowering plants. Recognise fertilisation in humans. Why do living things reproduce? How are characteristics passed on?

**Core concepts (teaching order)**
1. **Cells (1.1):** all living things are made up of cells. Under a microscope, higher magnification enlarges more (skin, leaf, onion root). ⚠ See flag 1.
2. **Why reproduce (1.2):** reproduction ensures the continuity of a kind. Examples: Le Le the panda, and the falling fertility rate.
3. **Heredity (1.3):** characteristics pass from parents to offspring, from both parents, so children are alike but not identical (counters model; earlobes, tongue rolling, dimples).
4. **Human reproduction (1.4):** testes make sperm cells and ovaries make egg cells. Fertilisation is one sperm cell fusing with one egg cell, in the fallopian tube. The baby develops in the uterus (womb).
5. **Flower parts (1.5A):** male: anther and filament. Female: stigma, style, ovary and ovules. Dissect and label.
6. **Pollination (1.5A):** pollen grains move from the anther to the stigma, carried by animals or wind. Flower features tell you which.
7. **Fertilisation in flowers:** the male reproductive cell from the pollen fuses with the egg cell in the ovule. The ovule becomes a seed and the ovary becomes a fruit.
8. **Seed dispersal (1.5B):** by wind, water, animals or splitting. It reduces overcrowding and competition for light, water and space.
9. **Germination (1.5C):** seeds need water, air and warmth, not light. This is the first full fair test of the year.
10. **The whole story (1.5D):** pollination → fertilisation → seed dispersal → germination.
11. **Compare (1.6):** humans and plants both have fertilisation; only plants have pollination.
12. **A plant that takes over (1.7):** a fast-spreading plant crowds out the others (reading a graph).

**Key terms:** cell, microscope, magnification, reproduce, continuity, offspring, characteristics, heredity, sperm (cell), egg (cell), testes, ovaries, fallopian tube, uterus (womb), fertilisation, anther, filament, stigma, style, ovary, ovule, pollen grains, (stamen, pistil ⚠), pollination, fruit, seed, seed dispersal, germination, variable changed / kept the same / measured.

**Common mistakes (evidence: CfU, AB 1.4 ideas, trigger bank, lab traps)**
- Mixing up pollination and fertilisation; putting seed dispersal before fertilisation; mixing up fruit and seed.
- The baby grows "in the stomach"; an egg is fertilised by "many sperms" (AB 1.4); characteristics come from the mother only.
- "Seeds need light to germinate."
- CfU tests: egg cells are made in the ovule (flower) and the ovary (human) (Q1); which parts can be removed and still give a fruit (Q2); pollination type from the flower's features (Q3); fair-test pair (Q4: B and C); dispersal from where the new plants grow (Q5); fruit features and the advantage of dispersal (Q6); light and germination, and seed leaves losing mass (Q7).
- Exam keywords: blocked fallopian tube → "may not meet"; no fruit → "fertilisation did not occur".

**Singapore examples:** Le Le, born at River Wonders on 14 Aug 2021 to Kai Kai and Jia Jia (⚠ Le Le moved to China in early 2025: verify and frame in the past). The Singapore Department of Statistics total-fertility-rate graph, 1988–2023 (⚠ resident rate 0.97 in 2023, below 1 for the first time: verify against the AB graph). Koster's Curse (AB p. 32 credits; NParks). School-garden flower walk; hibiscus and orchids. Durian flowers pollinated by bats ⚠. Angsana winged pods (wind), coconut and sea almond (water), love grass (hooks), rubber or balsam pods (splitting). Green beans (taugeh) for germination.

**Accuracy flags**
1. ⚠⚠ **Cells:** the syllabus outcome and AB 1.1 include cells, but BRIEF-P5 §5 and lab brief 1.1 say the teacher ruled cells outside this level. Ask before Episode 1.
2. ⚠⚠ **Trigger bank pp. 13–14** (mother's blood → baby's blood) borders the syllabus exclude "Foetal development and the umbilical cord mechanism". Keep to one sentence; no cord or placenta.
3. **Never** say self-/cross-pollination or pollen tube, or teach vegetative propagation (excluded).
4. Teacher's "main reproductive parts" list (anther, stigma, ovary, filament) omits the style and ovule. The AB labels all six.
5. ⚠ "stamen / pistil", "oviduct" and "heredity" are not in the AB text. Check the textbook.
6. Mind Lab "Pollen joins the ovule" is imprecise. Use the teacher's fertilisation line.
7. Positive language: lab brief 1.2 says "as older animals die". Use "after older members are gone"; pandas "at risk", not "dying out".

**Still open (needs the textbook):** chapter key inquiry questions; the textbook's flower diagram labels; the human-system labels in AB 1.4 (pictures only); the blank in AB 1.3 ("different" or "many" types); the fourth blank in AB 1.5B; CfU picture items (Q1–3, Q5, Q6 fruits).

## CHAPTER 2: CYCLES IN WATER (AB pp. 41–76)

**Key questions / outcomes.** Describe the water cycle. How does water change state when it gains or loses heat? What affects how fast water evaporates? Why must we conserve water and keep it clean?

**Core concepts (teaching order)**
1. **Three states** (P4 recap): ice, water, water vapour. **Melting (2.1):** ice gains heat and melts at 0 °C (melting point). The temperature stays at 0 °C while it melts: the flat part of the graph.
2. **Boiling (2.2):** water gains heat and boils at 100 °C (boiling point), throughout the liquid. The temperature stays at 100 °C. This is a teacher's demonstration; goggles.
3. **Freezing:** water loses heat and freezes at 0 °C.
4. **Condensation (2.3):** water vapour loses heat to a cooler surface and condenses. The droplets are clear even over coloured water, so they come from the air. Hot water gives droplets under the lid.
5. **Evaporation vs boiling:** surface vs throughout; any temperature vs the boiling point.
6. **Rate of evaporation (2.4):** temperature, exposed surface area and wind; paper towels on a balance; three fair tests.
7. **Water cycle (2.5):** evaporation → rising water vapour loses heat → condensation into clouds → droplets join → rain → back to seas and rivers. It keeps a continuous supply of water.
8. **Water for life (2.6):** life processes; conserving water.
9. **Pollution (2.7):** littering pollutes water bodies (Marina Reservoir).
10. **Water challenge (2.8):** a solar still uses evaporation and condensation; the water may still hold bacteria.

**Key terms:** solid, liquid, gas, water vapour, gains heat, loses heat, melting, melting point, freezing, boiling, boiling point, evaporation, condensation, water droplets, rate of evaporation, exposed surface area, wind, water cycle, clouds, rain, conserve, pollute.

**Common mistakes (evidence: CfU, trigger bank, recitation)**
- CfU tests: heat gain or loss for each process (Q1: freezing loss, melting gain, condensation loss); liquid to gas means boiling and evaporation (Q2); the temperature stays flat while water boils (Q3); fastest evaporation (Q4: highest temperature and largest area); fair-test pair (Q5: B and C); evaporation and condensation in a water-cycle diagram (Q6); where droplets form and why (Q7).
- "Droplets come from inside the can"; "the white mist is water vapour"; "evaporation only happens at boiling point"; "cold gets in".
- Answer frames: "Warmer water vapour from ( ) loses heat to cooler ( ) and condenses into water droplets"; "Liquid water gains heat from the surroundings and evaporates".

**Singapore examples:** a cold can of drink at the hawker centre "sweating"; iced kopi cups; teh-tarik steam; foggy spectacles leaving an air-conditioned mall; laundry on bamboo poles at HDB blocks (sun, wind, spread out); puddles drying at the void deck after rain. Marina Reservoir and the Marina Barrage; NEWater and the Four National Taps (PUB). Dengue and stagnant water (link to P3).

**Accuracy flags**
1. AB 2.2 says boiling water "changes into steam". Say "water vapour" and keep it invisible; the white mist is condensed droplets.
2. ⚠⚠ **Humidity:** BRIEF-P5 lists it as a factor, but AB 2.4 and the teacher name only three. Do not teach it until the teacher confirms.
3. Salt lowering the melting point (recitation) is outside the AB: enrichment only.
4. The trigger bank says "drinking water" for the solar still. Add the AB caution.
5. Lab brief 2.5 "the air gets cooler" is confusing. Say the water vapour loses heat to the cooler air higher up.
6. Boiling point 100 °C is the only figure; no talk of pressure or altitude.
7. Never particles or energy: "the heat is used for the change of state".

**Still open:** what the textbook calls the processes in its water-cycle diagram (for example, does it name "precipitation"?); "freezing point" as a term; the CfU Q3, Q6 and Q7 pictures; the content of Thematic Activity "Fun at the beach!".

## CHAPTER 3: PLANT TRANSPORT SYSTEM (AB pp. 79–100)

**Key questions / outcomes.** Recognise the transport of water and food in plants. What carries water, and what carries food? Which way does each move?

**Core concepts (teaching order)**
1. **What's inside a plant (3.1):** a plant without water wilts; water must travel from the roots to the leaves.
2. **The water path (3.2):** a celery stalk in coloured water. Coloured dots on the cut end are the water-carrying tubes; in a split stalk the colours do not mix. Water-carrying tubes carry water and mineral salts.
3. **What goes up and what comes down (3.3):** water and mineral salts go up from the roots; food made in the leaves goes to all parts in the food-carrying tubes. Cross-sections of a celery stalk and a sunflower leaf and stem (AB p. 87 credits).
4. **Design it (3.4):** a fair test on how much water a plant takes in.
5. **Help Mr Bala (3.5):** ⚠ probably ring removal. Food builds up above the ring, so the part above swells and fruit there grows bigger; a cut that goes too deep also stops water.

**Key terms:** roots, stem, leaves, water-carrying tubes, food-carrying tubes, mineral salts, transport, wilt, cross-section.

**Common mistakes:** saying xylem / phloem (excluded); "food goes up from the roots"; "food and water travel in the same tube"; "the dye travels in the food-carrying tubes"; thinking water goes straight from the roots to the flower without the stem's tubes; "plants digest food".

**Singapore examples:** white chrysanthemums or Chinese cabbage in coloured water (wet market); rambutan or mango orchards (ring cut for bigger fruit ⚠); NParks roadside trees with bark damaged by strimmers.

**Accuracy flags:** (1) xylem and phloem are excluded word for word; (2) lab brief 3.2 "warmer water climbs faster" is not P5 science, so drop it; (3) lab brief 3.5 "always kills the plant" breaks the positive-language rule; (4) the position of the tubes in a stem depends on the plant: check the textbook drawing; (5) photosynthesis details are P6, so say "food made in the leaves".

**Still open:** every Let's Conclude except AB 3.2's (p. 85: "There are [water-carrying] tubes, which transport [water] and mineral salts in plants"); the exact textbook names for the tubes; what 3.1, 3.4 and 3.5 really do; the CfU.

## CHAPTER 4: THE HUMAN RESPIRATORY AND CIRCULATORY SYSTEMS (AB pp. 101–136)

**Key questions / outcomes.** Identify the parts and functions of the respiratory and circulatory systems. How do plants, fish and humans take in oxygen and give out carbon dioxide? How do the two systems work together?

**Core concepts (teaching order)**
1. **What's in the air (4.1):** a mixture of gases, mostly nitrogen, with oxygen, carbon dioxide and water vapour.
2. **The respiratory system (4.2A):** nose → windpipe → lungs. In the lungs, oxygen passes into the blood and carbon dioxide passes out.
3. **Exhaled air (4.2B):** less oxygen, more carbon dioxide (limewater ⚠), more water vapour (a mirror fogs: Ch 2 link).
4. **Plants, fish and humans (4.3):** tiny openings on the leaves (mostly on the underside), gills, lungs. AB p. 114 credits a photo of a leaf's tiny openings.
5. **The circulatory system (4.4):** the heart pumps blood through blood vessels. Blood carries oxygen, digested food and water to every part, and carbon dioxide and waste away (P4 digestion link).
6. **Plants vs humans (4.5):** both transport substances; only humans have a pump.
7. **All in this together (4.6):** heart rate and breathing rate rise during exercise because the muscles need more oxygen and digested food.
8. **Journey of a drop of blood (4.7):** body → heart → lungs → heart → body.

**Key terms:** air, nitrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide, water vapour, nose, windpipe, lungs, breathe in / out, inhaled / exhaled air, gaseous exchange, gills, tiny openings, heart, blood vessels, blood, digested food, waste, heart rate, pulse, breathing rate.

**Common mistakes:** "we breathe in only oxygen and breathe out only carbon dioxide"; "exhaled air has no oxygen"; "fish breathe air with lungs"; "plants do not take in oxygen"; "blood carries air"; "air goes straight into the lungs"; "the heart beats faster to make more blood"; resting gives a higher heart rate (reading the 4.6 table).

**Singapore examples:** haze season and PSI readings (masks, staying indoors); the NAPFA 2.4 km run and a pulse check after it; HSA blood donation drives; S.E.A. Aquarium fish (gills); MRT cabin ventilation.

**Accuracy flags:** (1) ⚠⚠ "energy" in the heart-rate answer: exam schemes often want "more energy"; this app holds the word for P6, so the teacher decides; (2) "stomata" appears only in a photo credit: say "tiny openings"; (3) blood vessel types (arteries, veins, capillaries) are in no source, so leave them out; (4) lab brief 4.5 "digested food" in plants is wrong: say "food"; (5) the Mind Lab has no respiratory poster (the `respcirc` poster is titled "Circulatory System" and has only a "Lungs" branch); (6) plant photosynthesis gases are P6.

**Still open:** whether the textbook uses limewater, "air sacs", "mouth", "nostrils" or gas percentages; the 4.3 table answers; the CfU (an elderberry-leaf openings photo on AB p. 134).

## CHAPTER 5: ELECTRICAL SYSTEMS (AB pp. 137–152)

**Key questions / outcomes.** Identify the components of a simple circuit. When does a bulb light? Which materials let electric current through?

**Core concepts (teaching order)**
1. **Let's connect (5.1):** battery, crocodile-clip wires, a bulb and a model bulb. Pupils predict and test six set-ups. A bulb lights only when both of its contacts are joined to both battery terminals in one unbroken loop.
2. **Closed circuits (5.2):** closed = no gap, so electric current flows and the bulb lights. Open = a gap. A switch opens and closes the gap.
3. **Electrical conductors and insulators (5.3):** test objects with a circuit tester. Metals conduct; plastic, rubber, wood, glass and fabric insulate. A wire is a metal core in a plastic covering.
4. **Circuit diagrams (5.4):** standard symbols for battery, bulb, switch (open and closed) and wire.

**Key terms:** electric circuit, electric current, battery, terminal (+/−), wire, bulb, filament, switch, closed circuit, open circuit, electrical conductor, electrical insulator, circuit tester, circuit diagram, symbol.

**Common mistakes:** one wire is enough; touching a wire "near" the battery; "all shiny things conduct"; "the bulb uses up electricity"; drawing pictures instead of symbols; a fused bulb is still a closed circuit.

**Singapore examples:** torchlights and toy cars; the HDB kitchen's circuit breaker; Orchard Road Christmas lights; Singapore's 3-pin plugs (insulated plastic casing).

**Accuracy flags:** (1) "electric current": teacher's ruling 12 Oct 2026: the videos say "electric current" for what flows in a closed circuit and "electricity" for the supply; the Mind Lab poster's "Current flows" / "No current flows" / "Metals let current through" is being changed to "Electric current flows" / "No electric current flows" / "Metals let electric current through"; (2) say "battery", never "cell", to avoid a clash with Ch 1; (3) graphite is a non-metal conductor: check whether the textbook mentions it; (4) "not used up" (lab brief 5.2) is loose, so avoid it; (5) never "energy" or "voltage".

**Still open:** the AB TOC lists no CfU for Ch 5 (shared with Ch 6?); the textbook's exact definitions of the circuit terms; whether it says "electric current" (the videos do, per the 12 Oct 2026 ruling); the six circuits in 5.1.

## CHAPTER 6: SIMPLE SERIES AND PARALLEL ELECTRIC CIRCUITS (AB pp. 153–178)

**Key questions / outcomes.** Compare series and parallel circuits. How do the number and arrangement of batteries and bulbs change brightness? What happens when one bulb is removed? How do we use electricity safely and wisely?

**Core concepts (teaching order)**
1. **Battery train (6.1):** more batteries in series (+ to −) make the bulb brighter. A battery turned the wrong way works against the others.
2. **Bulbs in series (6.2):** one path; more bulbs make each bulb dimmer.
3. **Bulbs in parallel (6.3):** each bulb has its own path and stays as bright as one bulb alone.
4. **Different arrangements (6.4):** remove one bulb: in series all go out; in parallel the others stay lit. Homes are wired in parallel.
5. **Safely and wisely (6.5):** never touch with wet hands; avoid overloaded sockets and damaged wires; never poke objects into sockets. Switch off when not in use.
6. **Fun with circuits (6.6):** design a toy with a complete circuit and a switch.

**Key terms:** series circuit, parallel circuit, arrangement, brightness, brighter, dimmer, path, fused bulb, safety, overload.

**Common mistakes:** "parallel bulbs share the brightness"; "more bulbs make the circuit stronger"; "removing any bulb switches everything off"; batteries work whichever way round; "a device that is switched on but unused takes no electricity"; "brighter" said without a comparison.

**Singapore examples:** older fairy-light strings (series: one out, all out) vs home lighting (parallel); separate switches for each room in an HDB flat; ELCB / circuit breaker trips; SP Group bills (using less electricity).

**Accuracy flags:** (1) batteries in parallel are not in the AB TOC, so do not teach them; (2) parallel brightness must be stated against one bulb alone or against the same bulbs in series; (3) say "use less electricity", never "save energy"; (4) safety: batteries only, never the mains.

**Still open:** the 6.4 arrangements; the CfU (p. 173); the content of Thematic Activity "Working together!" (p. 179).
