## Big idea
Materials that let electric current flow through them are electrical conductors (metals); those that do not are electrical insulators (plastic, wood, rubber, glass); circuit diagrams use standard symbols for the battery, bulb, switch and wires.

## Misconceptions to bust
- "Shiny things are conductors." → Whether it conducts depends on the material; a shiny plastic ribbon is an electrical insulator (lab 5.3 written check; definitions 5.13 note "Trap: shiny means conductor"). **Becomes the `tip` beat.**
- "Hard things always let electric current flow." → Wood is hard and is an electrical insulator (lab 5.3 trap: "Yes. Hard things always let electricity flow across the gap."). **Wrong quiz option A.**
- "Every material lets electric current through, only more slowly." → An electrical insulator does not allow electric current to flow through it (lab 5.3 Challenge mix-up).
- "An object counts as a conductor whatever part touches the tester." → Only the part made of a conductor carries electric current across the gap (5.11, 5.12 applied; quiz options C and D).
- "A circuit diagram must look like a picture of the real parts." → It uses standard symbols (lab 5.4 trap; content_map Ch 5 "drawing pictures instead of symbols").
- "Plastic on a plug is there to look nice / to be safe." → Name the property: plastic is an electrical insulator (Mix-up buster #21; used again in EP 27).

## Pause and predict
**Q:** A spoon has a metal end and a wooden handle. Where must both wire ends of the tester touch for the bulb to light?
- A. Both on the wooden handle, because wood is hard. (lab 5.3 trap)
- B. Both on the metal end.
- C. One on the handle and one on the metal end.
- D. Anywhere, because it is a spoon.

**Answer: B.** Reason: metal is an electrical conductor, so it lets electric current flow across the gap; wood is an electrical insulator, so a wire end on the handle leaves a gap and the bulb stays dark.
