**How to use it:**

- **Takeaway** rows are the shaded one-line boxes that close each section. **WIHL** rows are the numbered "What I Have Learnt" summaries at the end of each chapter. These are the safest exam lines.

- The last column, **Monograph wording / note**, gives the monograph's wording where it states the same idea (✓ = agrees, ⚠ CONFLICT = disagrees or differs in scope). A dash (—) means the monograph does not state that idea, so the textbook wording stands on its own. Monograph wording is always set in guillemets, like «There are repeated patterns of change around us.», so it is never mixed up with textbook wording.
- Rows with a letter suffix (for example 2.27a) were added by the independent check; the original row numbers are unchanged.
- Every double-quoted string in this file is book text, and every one was machine-checked against the extracted book text (ignoring line breaks, curly/straight quotes, dash types and the book's own typos, which are marked [sic]). **Classification criteria used in P3 (from the pages above):**
- **Living vs non-living:** needs (water, food, air) and abilities (grow, respond to changes, reproduce).
- **Plant vs animal vs fungus vs bacterium:** makes own food when there is light (plants) / cannot make own food, depends on other living things (animals) / does not make own food, grows on another object, breaks it down and absorbs it, reproduces by spores (fungi) / tiny, different shapes and sizes (bacteria).
- **Flowering vs non-flowering plants:** bears flowers and reproduces from seeds in fruits / no flowers, reproduces from spores (may be in spore bags).
- **Where animals live (p. 23):** only on land, only in water, or both on land and in water (2.9a).
- **Animal groups:** outer covering, number of legs, body parts, how it breathes, where it lives, how it reproduces (eggs or young born alive), body temperature (cold-blooded reptiles, warm-blooded mammals). The book's order of the six groups is amphibians, birds, fish, insects, reptiles, mammals (alphabetical; same on the p. 24 tree and the p. 44 concept map).
- **Hedge words are part of the definition:** *usually*, *most* and *some* mark real exceptions (ostrich and penguin cannot fly; guppy gives birth; mudskipper lives on land; snakes have no legs; the platypus, named in the monograph, is a mammal that lays eggs). Never drop them.

**Magnet facts checklist (P3):**
- A magnet is usually made of iron or steel (6.2). Shapes in the book's order: bar, U-shaped, button, rod, ring, horseshoe.
- Magnetic materials: iron and steel (6.3). Non-magnetic: ceramic, fabric, glass, plastic, rubber, wood, and the metals aluminium and copper (6.4). Not all metals are magnetic.
- Two poles, North and South, where attraction is strongest (6.6–6.8).
- Unlike poles attract; like poles repel (6.14). Only a magnet can repel a magnet, so repulsion proves an object is a magnet (6.13). The monograph lists the two matching alternative conceptions: «all magnetic materials are magnets» and «objects are magnets if they can be attracted» (C4, p. 35).
- A freely suspended magnet comes to rest in the North–South direction, because Earth behaves like a magnet (6.16–6.17).
- Magnetise iron or steel by the stroke method (one pole, same direction; more strokes = stronger) or the electrical method (electromagnet; more turns or more batteries = stronger) (7.1–7.8).
- Concept map (p. 126): magnets have two poles (North and South); come to rest in the North–South direction when freely suspended; attract magnetic materials (iron, steel), which can be magnetised by the stroke method or electrical method to create an electromagnet; repel other magnets when their like poles face each other; attract other magnets when their unlike poles face each other; are used in everyday objects such as a refrigerator, doorstop and whiteboard.

### Conflicts and differences found (all also noted in the table rows)

1. **Comparing (2.4)** ⚠ CONFLICT. Book p. 20: similarities only. Monograph: always «similarities and differences» (C3 p. 31; Table 4 p. 19). The book's own p. 90 sentence (5.12) also says similarities and differences. **Rule:** teach comparing as finding what is the same and what is different.
2. **Classification wording (D0.1, 1.3)** ⚠ The book's pp. 3 and 5 lines leave out differences. Monograph Table 1 and book pp. 20 and 43 include them. **Rule:** use 2.33.
3. **Fungi and microscopes (2.27, 2.30)** ⚠ CONFLICT in scope. Monograph C1 (p. 21): «Bacteria and fungi are microscopic organisms that are not visible to our unaided eyes.» The book's fungi include mushrooms and bread mould you can see. **Rule:** never say *all fungi are microscopic*; bacteria need a microscope, mushrooms do not.
4. **Cycles question C1 (C0.4)** ⚠ minor wording difference (*makes a cycle* vs *makes up a cycle*). Use the book's.
5. **Physical properties (3.10)** ⚠ the monograph's classroom uses *hard* and *soft* as property words. Hardness is not one of the five P3 properties; keep it as an everyday word only.
6. **Mammals (2.22–2.23)** no conflict: the monograph's general statement is followed by the platypus exception, which is exactly why the book says *Most mammals give birth to their young alive*.
