## THEME OPENER: DIVERSITY (pp. 2–3)

- **Hook:** a visit to the zoo. Students list what they can see. Living things: children, animals, plants (rabbits and plants are "living things too"). Non-living things: water fountain, toy boat, bench, fence.
- **Interactions inside the scene:** living with living (children petting animals: Chen and Devi), living with non-living (children playing with a toy boat: Ali and Belle).
- **Big idea:** putting things with similar characteristics into groups is called **classification**. The zoo's contents can be split into two groups, living and non-living, and classifying helps us understand what we see.
- **Key inquiry questions (paraphrased):**
  - D1: What can we see around us?
  - D2: How can we group the huge variety of living and non-living things?
  - D3: Why does keeping diversity matter?

## CHAPTER 1: DIVERSITY OF LIVING AND NON-LIVING THINGS (Theme: Diversity, pp. 4–18)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
- What can we observe around us?
- What do living things need?
- What can living things do?
- **Opener (p. 4):** a picture of a bedroom. List everything, compare lists with classmates, then find a way to group the things and a way to present the groups.

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Diversity (p. 5):** the great variety of living and non-living things around us is called **diversity**. To understand it, we group things by their **similarities** (characteristics they have in common).
2. **Classification (p. 5):** grouping things by their similarities is called classification. (Three slightly different wordings appear in the book: p. 3, p. 5, and pp. 20/43. See `definitions.md`; use the p. 43 summary wording in scripts.)
3. **Presenting a classification (Let's Think and Do: "Classifying", pp. 5–6):** three ways:
   - **pictures** (bedroom items sorted into two picture groups),
   - **a table** (two columns: living = flowers, boy, cat; non-living = bed, books, table, chair, pencils, ruler, pencil case, toy car, toy bear),
   - **a graphic organiser** ("Things around us" branching into living and non-living, with bullet lists).
4. **Open question about viruses (p. 8):** COVID-19 is caused by a virus. Are viruses living? Scientists have debated this for over 100 years. The book leaves it open and uses it to motivate "what are the characteristics of living things?".
5. **Living things need water, food and air (pp. 9–13).** The cat, ant, plant and mushrooms all need these to survive.
6. **Water (pp. 9–10):**
   - Wilting-plant story: a student calls a drooping plant "sad"; Mr Bala explains it is **wilting** because it lacks water and could die if not watered. After watering it stands tall again.
   - Humans need water to digest food, to carry out daily activities and to remove waste from the body. Plants need water to make food.
7. **Food (p. 11):** all living things need food because food gives them **energy** (a child is hungry after using a lot of energy in PE). People eat rice, meat, vegetables and fruit; cows graze on grass; plants do not eat, but make their own food when there is light.
8. **Air (p. 13):**
   - Land animals cannot stay underwater for long because they do not have body parts that let them breathe underwater (a boy snorkelling with a mask).
   - Living things breathe in air that contains **oxygen**. Without air, plants and animals die.
   - A grasshopper kept in a box needs holes in the box so that air can get in.
9. **Living things grow (p. 14):** they get bigger and also become **more complex** (a baby becomes a toddler with more hair and longer limbs). Growth stages of a person: baby, child, teenager, adult. Animals eat food to grow. Plants do not need to eat to grow; they make their own food using light, and like animals they increase in size and become more complex.
10. **Living things respond to changes in the environment (p. 15):**
    - Our eyelids shut at once when bright light shines in, which stops too much light from entering and damaging the eyes. Responding keeps us safe.
    - A plant near a window grows towards sunlight (responding to light).
    - The mimosa ("touch-me-not") folds its leaves inwards and droops when touched, to protect itself, and reopens after a few minutes.
11. **Living things reproduce (pp. 16–17):**
    - Reproducing makes more of the same kind and is needed for survival. Living things that cannot survive become **extinct** (they all die out).
    - Some animals give birth to live young; others lay eggs from which young hatch.
    - Numbers: humans usually have one baby at a time; rabbits one to 12 young; a female mosquito lays 100 to 200 eggs at a time. Not all eggs hatch, but many young still result.
    - Plants reproduce by **seeds** (a mature tomato plant and its seeds) or by **spores** (a fern and its spores).

### Real-world / Singapore contexts
- Zoo visit (theme opener); bedroom; school PE lesson; family lunch.
- **Thirst (p. 10):** feeling thirsty can mean the body needs more water, for example after sweating or urinating more than usual. People can generally survive without water for up to about three days.
- **Food security and food waste (p. 12):** in Singapore most people get food easily from supermarkets and hawker centres, but elsewhere war, bad weather or sickness can stop food from arriving (the World Food Programme reported about 283 million people needing food urgently in 2021). Singapore's household and food-industry waste reached about 665,000 tonnes in 2020 (the book compares it with 22.2 million ten-year-olds at 30 kg each). Tips: ask for smaller portions, order for the right number of guests, bring a container for leftovers, shop with a list.
- **Mount Everest (p. 13):** climbers carry oxygen tanks because there is very little oxygen in the air near the top.
- **Mosquitoes and stagnant water (p. 17):** do not leave stagnant water at home, so mosquitoes cannot reproduce so quickly (links forward to dengue in Chapter 5).

### Misconceptions / exam points
- **Living things need water, food and air** (all three, for plants too). Use the three words together.
- **Living things grow, respond to changes and reproduce** (the three things living things can do).
- **Plants do not eat.** They make their own food when there is light. Avoid "plants eat sunlight / soil".
- A plant is not "sad": it is **wilting** because it lacks water. (The book models correcting a feeling word into a science word.)
- Growth means increasing in size **and** becoming more complex, not only getting bigger.
- A teddy bear (non-living) does not grow (p. 75, a child's question in Chapter 4).
- Responding to changes protects living things (eyelids, mimosa); plants respond too, just more slowly (growing towards light).
- Reproduction is about the survival of the kind; a kind that cannot survive becomes extinct.
- Not every egg hatches, which is why some animals lay many eggs.
- Viruses are an open question in the book; do not state that they are living or non-living.
- No Let's Apply section in this chapter.

## CHAPTER 2: CLASSIFICATION OF LIVING THINGS (Theme: Diversity, pp. 19–44)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
- What are the similarities and differences between plants and animals?
- What other groups of living things are there?
- **Opener (p. 19):** Carolus Linnaeus (Sweden, 1707–1778), curious about plant names as a boy, went on a six-month trip at 25, described over 100 previously unknown plants, and with his students developed a classification system still used today. Prompts: what qualities made him successful, and how did he classify what he saw?

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Why classify (p. 20):** scientists group the huge variety of living things so they can study them systematically. Classification = grouping living things by their similarities and differences.
2. **Binomial nomenclature (Science Around Us, p. 20):** Linnaeus's naming system; "binomial" means two parts, so each living thing has a two-word name (humans are *Homo sapiens*).
3. **Four main groups of living things (p. 20):** **plants, animals, fungi and bacteria**. A fifth group (protists) is named as "for the future".
4. **Comparing (Let's Think and Do: "Classifying and Comparing", p. 20):** the book defines comparison as looking for similarities between two things (see the accuracy flag in Misconceptions).
5. **Plants (p. 21):** living things that usually have leaves, stems and roots and can make their own food when there is light. Parts labelled on a plant diagram: flower, leaf, fruit, stem, root.
6. **Flowering and non-flowering plants (pp. 21–22):**
   - **Flowering plants** bear flowers and reproduce from seeds; the flowers develop into fruits, and the seeds are inside the fruits. Examples: hibiscus, water hyacinth (photo of a papaya cut open for seeds in a fruit).
   - **Non-flowering plants** do not bear flowers; they reproduce from spores, which may be found in **spore bags**. Examples: bird's nest fern, water clover.
7. **Animals (p. 23):** living things that usually have a head, a body and limbs; they cannot make their own food and depend on other living things for food. Some live only on land (lion), some only in water (fish), some on land and in water (frog).
8. **Outer coverings (p. 24):** animals have different outer coverings because they live in different places, for example the chameleon's dry, scaly covering keeps it from drying out in very dry places. Scientists group animals by similarities and differences into **amphibians, birds, fish, insects, reptiles and mammals**.
9. **Amphibians (p. 25):** can live both on land and in water; usually four legs; strong legs and webbed feet for swimming; breathe through their skin as well as their lungs; protective moist skin stops them drying out on land; lay eggs in water. Frog eggs hatch into tadpoles that live in water; adult frogs can live on land. Examples: frog, salamander, toad.
10. **Birds (pp. 26–27):** live largely on land; beaks for picking up food; wings, and usually can fly (ostriches and penguins cannot); outer covering of **feathers**; lay eggs, some of which hatch into chicks. Egg sizes differ (ostrich, goose, turkey, duck, chicken, quail). Examples: parrot, penguin, ostrich.
11. **Fish (pp. 27–28):** usually live only in water; **fins** to swim; outer covering of **scales**; usually breathe through **gills** (diagram labels: fins, scales, gills, tail). Some lay many eggs at one time (cod); others give birth to live young (guppy, some sharks). Mudskippers are special fish that can live on land and in water (gills in water, skin on land). Examples: sea bass, guppy.
12. **Insects (pp. 29–30):** probably among the most common animals (about 10 quintillion alive at any moment); most live on land. **Six legs and a pair of feelers**; body divided into **head, thorax and abdomen**; many adults have two pairs of wings, some one pair, some none; outer covering usually hard and shell-like. Most lay many tiny, easily destroyed eggs so that some hatch (a cockroach laying eggs). Examples: cricket, grasshopper, mosquito, ant.
13. **Reptiles (p. 31):** some live on land, some in water (snakes, turtles, crocodiles, lizards); most have four legs, except snakes; usually dry, scaly skin; breathe through lungs; most lay eggs. **Cold-blooded:** their body temperature is not constant like ours but changes with their surroundings.
14. **Mammals (p. 32):** some live on land, some in water; **hair or fur** as outer covering; most give birth to live young; females have **mammary glands** that make milk for the young. **Warm-blooded:** they keep a constant body temperature, helped by structures that produce sweat when it is hot. Examples: monkey, cat, human, bat (all have hair).
15. **Takeaway (p. 33):** plants and animals can be classified by common observable characteristics; there are different types of each.
16. **Fungi (pp. 34–35):** scientists found living things that did not fit plants or animals (mould, mushrooms, yeasts); because they reproduce by spores and do not make their own food, a new group was made. Fungi (singular **fungus**) do not make their own food; they grow on another object, release a substance that breaks it down into simpler substances, and absorb these as food (bread mould on bread). They reproduce quickly through spores; a mushroom's spores are in the **gills** under the **cap**, and wind disperses them (parts: cap, stalk, gills).
    - **Useful fungi:** edible mushrooms (food), yeast (makes bread dough rise), tempeh (soybeans broken down by a white fungus; rich in protein).
    - **Harmful fungi:** bread mould; eating mouldy bread can make us sick.
17. **Bacteria (pp. 36–38):** found as microscopes became more powerful; living things even smaller than fungi. Bacteria (singular **bacterium**) are tiny living things of different shapes and sizes: **rod-shaped, sphere-shaped (round), spiral-shaped**.
    - **Harmful bacteria:** cause sickness; many are on our hands though we cannot see them, so wash hands before eating.
    - **Useful bacteria:** bacteria in our body help break down food; yoghurt and yoghurt drinks contain such bacteria.
18. **Takeaway (p. 39):** fungi and bacteria are living things too.

### Misconceptions / exam points
- **The four groups:** plants, animals, fungi, bacteria. Fungi are **not plants** (they do not make their own food; they reproduce by spores). Mushrooms are fungi.
- **Spores are not only for fungi:** non-flowering plants (ferns) also reproduce by spores. The difference is food: plants make their own food when there is light; fungi do not.
- **Flowering plants reproduce from seeds found in fruits**; flowers develop into fruits.
- **Not all birds can fly** (ostrich, penguin), so "has wings" or "has feathers" is the safer bird characteristic than "can fly".
- **Not all fish lay eggs** (guppy, some sharks give birth to live young), and **not all fish live only in water** (mudskipper).
- **A sea star is not a fish:** it has no scales or gills. (Accuracy flag: the book also says sea stars "do not lay eggs". Sea stars do release eggs, so avoid repeating that line; use the scales-and-gills reason only.)
- **Insect checklist:** six legs, one pair of feelers, three body parts (head, thorax, abdomen); wings vary (two pairs, one pair or none), so wings are not a reliable test.
- **Amphibians** breathe through skin and lungs and lay eggs in water; reptiles have dry scaly skin and breathe through lungs only. Keep amphibian (moist skin) and reptile (dry scaly skin) apart.
- **Snakes are reptiles** even though they have no legs.
- **Bats are mammals** (they have hair), not birds.
- **Most mammals** give birth to live young (the book says "most"); the defining features in the book are hair or fur, mammary glands and milk.
- **Cold-blooded / warm-blooded** are the book's terms (reptiles cold-blooded; mammals warm-blooded). Cold-blooded means body temperature changes with the surroundings, not that the animal feels cold.
- **Sweat (accuracy note):** the book says mammals keep a constant body temperature and have structures that produce sweat when it is hot. Keep it as the book says ("mammals have structures that produce sweat"), but do not extend it to "every mammal sweats to cool down" (dogs mostly pant, for example).
- **Outer coverings as the classification clue:** feathers (birds), scales (fish), dry scaly skin (reptiles), moist skin (amphibians), hair or fur (mammals), hard shell-like covering (insects).
- **Not all bacteria or fungi are harmful.** Name one useful and one harmful example of each.
- **Comparing accuracy flag:** p. 20 defines comparison as looking for similarities between two things, but every comparing task in the book (pp. 41, 53, 90–92) asks for similarities **and** differences. Scripts should teach "find what is the same and what is different".
- **Classification wording:** use "grouping things based on their similarities and differences" (p. 43).
- **Let's Apply (pp. 40–42):**

  - Q2: two animals; state one similarity and one difference based on characteristics. ## CHAPTER 3: DIVERSITY OF MATERIALS (Theme: Diversity, pp. 45–70)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
- What are the different types of materials around us?
- What are the physical properties of materials?
- How are materials with different properties used?
- **Opener (p. 45):** a child wants a food container for recess snacks; the kitchen has containers made of plastic, metal, cardboard, glass and ceramic. Containers made of different materials serve different functions, so choose the right one for the food.

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Seven materials (p. 46):** objects are made from different materials: **wood, metal, ceramic, rubber, glass, plastic and fabric**.
2. **Wood (p. 46):** comes from trees; trunks and branches are cut into long, flat planks. Used for furniture, musical instruments, sports equipment and paper. Paper-making (Science Around Us): wood is mashed into pulp, water is added, and the mixture is made into sheets and dried.
3. **Metal (p. 47):** dug up from the ground. Gold (jewellery), iron (gates and railings), copper (electrical wires, cooking pots), aluminium (drink cans, window frames, aeroplanes).
4. **Ceramic (p. 48):** made from **clay**, which is found in soil. It is shaped, then dried or fire-baked in a hot oven called a **kiln**; heating hardens it, but it can break easily. Used for vases, bowls, plates, cups, bricks, toilets and even man-made teeth.
5. **Rubber (p. 49):** from the **sap** of a rubber tree, or man-made from oil from the ground. Used for rain boots, bicycle tyres, rubber bands, bouncing balls, raincoats and rubber gloves.
6. **Glass (p. 50):** made from **sand** heated until it melts and becomes clear; poured into moulds and cooled into clear glass. Because we can see through it: glass tanks, window panes, light bulbs, spectacles. Glass blowers shape very hot glass.
7. **Plastic (p. 51):** made in factories from oil from deep underground. Bottles, bags, shoes, pens, toothbrushes. Usually **long-lasting** (takes a long time to break down), so reuse and recycle to cut plastic waste.
8. **Fabric (p. 52):** comes from animals or plants: **wool** from sheep (sweaters to keep warm), **silk** from a kind of caterpillar (scarves, clothing, bedsheets), **cotton** from a plant (shirts, towels). Read the fabric label inside your clothes.
9. **Physical properties (p. 53):** each material has its own physical properties, which can be measured or observed, and which let us tell materials apart and choose the right material for an object. The five taught: **strength, flexibility, ability to float or sink in water, waterproof, transparency**. (Let's Think and Do: "Comparing": compare properties to see similarities and differences.)
10. **Strength (pp. 53–54):** a material's ability to be pulled or pushed without breaking or tearing.
    - **Strong** materials do not break easily when dropped, hit with a hammer or pulled apart; materials that are **not strong** break easily when dropped, pulled or knocked.
    - Paired examples: metal wire is stronger than cotton thread; a plastic plate is stronger than a paper plate; a ceramic plate is **fragile** but a rubber plate is not; a glass cup is fragile but a metal cup is strong.
    - A property can change with how a material is treated: wood needs an axe to break it, but wood made into paper tears by hand.
11. **Flexibility (p. 55):** a material's ability to bend without breaking.
    - **Flexible:** bends easily without breaking (rubber tile). **Rigid:** breaks easily when bent (ceramic tile).
    - Flexibility comes in degrees: rubber tube bends more than plastic tube, which bends more than metal tube, so rubber is the most flexible and metal the least of the three.
12. **Ability to float or sink in water (p. 56):** the material affects whether an object floats or sinks. Metal block and metal spoon sink (most metals sink); wooden block and wooden spoon float (most types of wood float). Metal ships float because of the large amount of **trapped air** inside.
13. **Waterproof (p. 57):** some materials absorb water and let water pass through; others do not. Materials that do not absorb water at all are **waterproof**: rubber, plastic, glass and metals (they do not absorb water or let it pass through). Fabric absorbs water and lets it pass through, so it is not waterproof (coffee strainer), but it can be made waterproof with a layer of wax (waxed shoes).
14. **Transparency (p. 58):** how much light can pass through a material.
    - **Transparent:** lets most light through; objects behind are seen clearly (glass, some plastics).
    - **Translucent:** lets only some light through; objects behind are not seen clearly (frosted glass, some plastics).
    - **Opaque:** lets no light through; objects behind cannot be seen (rubber, wood, metal).
15. **Objects and purposes (p. 59):** materials are used to make objects; objects are things used for a purpose. Table: bath towel absorbs water (fabric); scissors cut (metal); raincoat keeps you dry in rain (plastic); water hose carries water (rubber).
16. **Properties decide uses (pp. 60–62):** each property suits some objects but not others.
    - Metal trolley: rigid (holds heavy groceries without bending) and strong (does not break when knocked). A glass or ceramic trolley would be unsuitable.
    - Fabric curtains: flexible (fold at the side); some fabrics are opaque (block sunlight).
    - Glass fish tank: transparent (see the fish and plants) and waterproof (no leaks).
    - Rubber gloves: waterproof and flexible.
    - Ceramic floor tiles: described as strong (do not break unless hit by a heavy object; see the accuracy flag below).
    - Plastic water bottles: waterproof and do not break easily when dropped.
    - Wooden furniture: rigid, so it holds things placed on it.
17. **One object, different materials (p. 63):** the choice depends on the use. A home drinking-water container can be plastic, glass or metal (all waterproof). Clear plastic and glass are transparent (you can see when it is empty); metal is not. Glass is not as strong as plastic or metal and breaks when dropped, so handle it carefully. A table is wood because wood is strong; what else could be used?
18. **Many materials in one object (pp. 64–65):** an object such as a car has parts with different purposes, and each part's material is chosen for its properties: rubber tyres (strong, withstand knocks, hold large weights); fabric seats (flexible, shape to passengers); glass windscreen (protects from wind and rain; the book's reason is waterproof); metal body (strong, protects in an accident).
19. **The design process (p. 66):** engineers choose materials by their properties, and scientists create new materials. Steps: think about what the object is for; list the parts needed; decide the intended use of each part; draw the design; choose materials whose properties suit each part. Task: design a school bag.

### Misconceptions / exam points
- **The five P3 properties:** strength, flexibility, ability to float or sink in water, waterproof, transparency. Hardness, conducting heat and magnetism are not in this chapter (heat conduction is P4; magnetism is Chapter 6).
- **Answer pattern for "why is X made of Y?":** name the property, then link it to the object's function (Let's Apply Q1 hint: what does the object do, and how does the property help?). Example structure: "glass is transparent, so light passes through and the user can see clearly".
- **Flexible vs rigid:** rigid means it breaks easily when bent (the book's definition). Rigid is also used to mean "does not bend under a load" (metal trolley, wooden furniture). Use the book's wording.
- **Strong vs fragile:** strength is about being pulled or pushed without breaking or tearing; fragile materials (glass, ceramic) break easily when dropped or knocked.
- **Waterproof** means does not absorb water at all (and does not let water through). Fabric is not waterproof unless treated (wax).
- **Transparent / translucent / opaque** depend on how much light passes through: most / some / none. Glass and some plastics transparent; frosted glass and some plastics translucent; rubber, wood, metal opaque. "Some plastics" appear in two groups, so the material alone does not decide; the specific object does.
- **Metals sink but ships float:** trapped air.
- **Treatment changes properties:** wood (strong) compared with paper (tears easily); fabric made waterproof with wax.
- **Same object, different materials; one object, many materials.**
- **Accuracy flags (book inconsistencies to handle carefully):**
  - **Ceramic:** "can break easily" (p. 48), "fragile" (p. 54) and "rigid, breaks when bent" (p. 55), but "strong, does not break easily unless hit by a heavy object" for floor tiles (p. 62).   - **Car windscreen (p. 65):** the book's reason is that glass is waterproof (wind and rain protection). It does not mention transparency. If a script mentions transparency too, keep the book's waterproof reason in the exam wording.
  - **Table (p. 63) vs furniture (p. 62):** wood is called "strong" for the table and "rigid" for furniture. Both are book-accurate.

## CHAPTER 5: LIFE CYCLES OF ANIMALS (Theme: Cycles, pp. 80–98)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
- What are the stages in the life cycles of different animals?
- How are the life cycles of animals similar or different?
- **Opener (p. 80), "Save the Butterflies":** Mr Bala tells Ali that some butterflies in Singapore are at risk of dying out because there are not enough plants to lay their eggs on. What happens if fewer eggs are laid? (Answered on p. 97.)

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Belle's park walk (p. 81):** chickens, grasshoppers, a frog on a lotus leaf and tadpoles in a pond. How do these animals change as they grow from young to adult?
2. **Three-stage life cycles (pp. 82–85): egg → young → adult.** Some young look like their parents; some do not.
   - **Chicken (p. 82):** egg laid by the adult; young = **chick**, lives on land and looks like the adult; adult lives on land.
   - **Frog (p. 83):** egg laid by the adult; young = **tadpole**, lives in water, has a tail and does not look like the adult; adult lives on land and in water. Frog eggs have a **jelly-like covering** that protects them from being eaten and from drying out.
   - **Grasshopper (p. 84):** egg laid by the adult; young = **nymph**, lives on land, looks like the adult but has no wings, and moults several times as it grows into an adult; adult lives on land, has wings and can fly.
   - **Moulting (Science Around Us, p. 84):** as a young insect grows bigger, it sheds its outer skin and grows a new one.
   - **Cockroach (p. 85):** eggs are laid in an **egg case**; young = nymph (looks like the adult, no wings, moults several times); adult lives on land, has wings and can fly.
3. **Four-stage life cycles (pp. 86–88): egg → larva → pupa → adult.** The young do not look like the adult.
   - **Mealworm beetle (p. 86):** egg laid by the adult; **larva** lives on land, does not look like the adult, eats a lot and moults many times; **pupa** does not eat, does not look like the adult, and develops into an adult inside the **pupal case**; adult lives on land, has wings and can fly.
   - **Butterfly (p. 87):** egg laid on a leaf; larva = **caterpillar** (lives on land, eats a lot, moults many times); pupa (does not eat, develops in the pupal case); adult (lives on land, wings, flies). Caterpillars have six legs like all insects, plus five more pairs of **prolegs** (false legs that help them move), which they lose as adults.
   - **Mosquito (p. 88):** egg laid in **stagnant water**; larva = **wriggler**, lives near the water surface, eats a lot, moults many times; pupa lives near the water surface, does not eat, develops in the pupal case; adult lives on land, has wings and can fly. More than 180 mosquito species are known in Singapore; most do not spread disease, but *Aedes aegypti* and *Aedes albopictus* spread dengue.
4. **Dengue (Let's Explore, p. 89):** dengue fever is spread by infected mosquitoes such as *Aedes aegypti*; higher temperatures and rainfall make mosquitoes breed faster, so dengue cases rise. Remove breeding spots at school and home (NEA "Stop Dengue with B-L-O-C-K" / "Mozzie Wipeout").
5. **Summary idea (p. 89):** animals have three or four stages. Three stages: egg, young, adult. Four stages: egg, larva, pupa, adult.
6. **Comparing life cycles (Let's Think and Do: "Comparing", pp. 90–91):** compare using observable characteristics: the **number of stages** and the **physical features** of the animals (does the young look like the adult?).
   - Frog vs grasshopper: **similar** because both have three stages; **different** because the tadpole does not look like the adult frog, while the grasshopper nymph does look like the adult.
   - Frog vs mosquito: **similar** because in both the young do not look like the adults; **different** because the frog has three stages and the mosquito four.
7. **Takeaway (p. 92):** compare life cycles by the stages and by the characteristics of the young and the adult.
8. **No first or last stage (p. 93):** Devi asks whether day or night comes first, and whether the chicken's life cycle begins with the egg. Her father explains day and night repeat with no start or end because of Earth's rotation, and life-cycle stages repeat too, with no first or last stage.
9. **Answer to the opener (p. 97):** with too few plants to lay eggs on, there are fewer larvae, then fewer pupae and adult butterflies. Life cycles let living things of the same kind continue to live on Earth, so what can we do to save the butterflies?

### Misconceptions / exam points
- **Stage names:** three-stage = egg, young, adult; four-stage = egg, larva, pupa, adult. Name the young correctly: chick, tadpole, nymph (grasshopper and cockroach), caterpillar (butterfly larva), wriggler (mosquito larva).
- **A tadpole is the young of a frog**, not a larva in this book's model (the frog is a three-stage cycle: egg, young, adult). Do not call the frog a four-stage animal.
- **Looks like the adult:** chick, grasshopper nymph and cockroach nymph do; tadpole, larva and pupa do not.
- **Nymphs have no wings** but otherwise look like the adult; they moult several times.
- **The pupa does not eat** and is not "dead" or "sleeping": it is developing into the adult inside the pupal case.
- **The larva eats a lot and moults many times**; the larva is the growing stage.
- **Caterpillars are insects** with six legs; prolegs are false legs.
- **Mosquito larva and pupa live near the water surface** (in water); the adult lives on land. This is the usual "difference" against the mealworm beetle, whose larva lives on land (the book does not say where the beetle egg and pupa are).
- **No first or last stage** in a cycle; the arrows form a loop.
- Most mosquitoes do not spread disease; dengue is spread by infected *Aedes* mosquitoes.
- **Comparison answers** must give the basis: "both have three stages" or "the young of A looks like the adult but the young of B does not".

## CHAPTER 6: PROPERTIES OF MAGNETS (Theme: Interactions, pp. 100–112)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
- What is a magnet?
- Which parts of a magnet have the strongest pull?
- How do magnets interact with one another?
- What direction does a freely suspended magnet point in?
- **Opener (p. 100):** Devi's toy globe floats above its base, and the box says a magnet makes it float. How? (Answered on p. 112: interactions between magnets.) Magnets are found in many things around us.

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Discovery story (Science Around Us, p. 101):** the shepherd Magnes found that the metal tip of his walking stick was attracted to a natural magnet, a **lodestone**. Lodestones led to a compass-like tool that always points North–South, used by sailors and hikers.
2. **What a magnet is (p. 102):** an object usually made of **iron or steel**. Shapes: **bar, U-shaped, horseshoe, button, rod, ring**.
3. **Magnetic materials (p. 102):** magnets **attract** objects made of magnetic materials, which include **iron and steel** (iron nails and steel pins).
4. **Non-magnetic materials (p. 103):** objects not attracted to magnets are made of non-magnetic materials: ceramic, fabric, glass, plastic, rubber and wood. **Aluminium and copper are metals that are non-magnetic.** Examples: cotton towel, aluminium foil, wooden ruler, ceramic bowl, eraser, copper wire, plastic bottle, glass cup.
5. **Classifying materials with a magnet (p. 104):** one way to classify materials is to see how they interact with a magnet (cotton T-shirt, wooden pencil, ceramic plate, plastic bag, glass cup, iron nail, rubber tyre). Takeaway: a magnet can interact with objects made of magnetic materials.
6. **Poles (p. 105):** a magnet has two poles, a **North pole** and a **South pole** (shown on U-shaped, ring and bar magnets).
7. **Strongest at the poles (p. 106):** a bar magnet held over steel clips attracts the most clips at its two poles, so the pull (attraction) is strongest there. Try a rod or U-shaped magnet too.
8. **Attract and repel (pp. 107–108):** magnets can attract (pull) or repel (push) one another, depending on which poles face each other.
   - **Unlike poles attract:** a North pole attracts the South pole of another magnet.
   - **Like poles repel:** North repels North; South repels South.
   - Iron powder sprinkled around magnets shows patterns: the **magnetic field** (Science Around Us, p. 108), different for like and unlike poles facing.
9. **Ring magnets floating (p. 109):** stacked ring magnets on a rod float above one another because facing like poles repel.
10. **Testing for a magnet (p. 109):** a magnet can attract or repel another magnet; you can confirm an object is a magnet if it **repels** another magnet. Takeaway: unlike poles attract and like poles repel.
11. **The compass (pp. 110–111):**
    - Ancient Chinese traders used a compass whose needle was a magnetic spoon; the tip of the spoon points South.
    - A compass needle, when it stops moving, always points along the North–South direction of the Earth (compass rose N, E, S, W).
    - **Earth behaves like a magnet** with its own North and South poles; it interacts with a **freely suspended magnet**, which therefore always points North–South and can be used to tell direction. A freely suspended magnet comes to rest in the North–South direction.
12. **Answer to the opener (p. 112):** the toy globe floats because of interactions between magnets.

### Misconceptions / exam points
- **Not all metals are magnetic.** Iron and steel are magnetic; aluminium and copper are not. "Magnets attract all metals" is wrong.
- **Magnets do not repel non-magnetic materials;** they simply do not attract them. Repelling only happens between magnets.
- **Attraction does not prove an object is a magnet** (a magnet also attracts a plain iron nail). **Repulsion proves it.**
- **The pull is strongest at the poles**, not the middle.
- **Every magnet has two poles** (North and South), whatever its shape (ring and U-shaped included).
- **Unlike poles attract and like poles repel** (say "unlike"/"like", not "opposite"/"same", in the exam wording; the book uses unlike/like).
- **A freely suspended magnet comes to rest in the North–South direction** because Earth behaves like a magnet. Use "comes to rest" and "freely suspended".
- **The toy globe and ring magnets float because of repulsion** between like poles.
- **Spoon compass (accuracy note, p. 110):** the book says the "tip" of the spoon points South. In the historical Chinese spoon compass it is the handle end that points South, so in scripts say "the spoon settles pointing North–South" rather than naming an end.
- **Compass needle vs freely suspended magnet:** both come to rest North–South because Earth behaves like a magnet; the compass needle is itself a magnet.

## CHAPTER 7: MAKING AND USING MAGNETS (Theme: Interactions, pp. 113–126)

### Key questions / learning outcomes
- How can we make a magnet?
- How are magnets used in our everyday life?
- **Opener (p. 113):** Belle asks Ali whether an iron nail can be made into a magnet. How would you know you had made one?

### Core concepts (in teaching order)
1. **Magnetising (p. 114):** a magnetic object can be made into a magnet by the **stroke method** or the **electrical method**; once it becomes a magnet it has been **magnetised**.
2. **Stroke method (p. 114):** stroke the magnetic object with a strong magnet.
   - Step 1: stroke the iron nail with **one pole** of a bar magnet.
   - Step 2: repeat the strokes **in the same direction** each time, always with **the same pole**.
   - Step 3: test the nail near steel clips to see whether it attracts any.
   - More strokes make a stronger magnet that attracts more steel clips.
3. **Electrical method (p. 115):** the magnet made this way is an **electromagnet**.
   - Step 1: coil a long piece of wire around an iron nail.
   - Step 2: connect the ends of the wire to a battery.
   - Step 3: test the nail near steel clips.
   - **Safety warning:** do not leave it connected to the battery too long (things may get hot); do not touch exposed wires with bare hands; never connect it to the mains electrical supply.
4. **Making an electromagnet stronger (pp. 116–117):** increase the **number of turns of wire** around the nail (20 turns attracts more clips than ten), or increase the **number of batteries** (two attract more than one).
5. **Takeaway (p. 117):** objects made of iron and steel can be magnetised by either method.
6. **Fair test (Let's Think and Do: "Designing investigations", p. 118):** experiments answer a question or test a prediction. In a **fair test**, only one **variable** is changed and the others are kept the same; otherwise we cannot tell which variable explains the result. Repeat the experiment a few times so the results are **reliable**.
   - **Prediction:** more batteries → a stronger electromagnet.
   - **Variable to be changed:** number of batteries.
   - **Variables to be kept the same (examples):** type of batteries, type of steel clips, type of iron nail, number of turns of wire.
   - **Variable to be measured:** number of steel clips attracted.
7. **Everyday uses (pp. 119–122):**
   - **Refrigerator door:** magnetic strips inside keep the door closed.
   - **Scrapyard crane:** a large iron disc becomes a powerful electromagnet that picks out iron and steel from other materials.
   - **Magnetic whiteboard:** a steel sheet under the writing surface lets magnets stick.
   - **Magnetic doorstop:** holds the door in place and stops it closing suddenly.
   - **Maglev train:** magnets on the train and the track repel, so the train "floats" above the track and can travel very fast.
   - **MRI scanner (Science Around Us):** a large round magnet with a tunnel; it takes detailed pictures of the inside of the body to find problems; safe and painless.
   - **Toys and gadgets:** magnetic window cleaner, magnetic fishing game, magnetic note holder.
8. **Interaction summary (p. 121, speech bubble):** magnets attract other magnets or magnetic objects and can repel each other; this is magnets interacting with one another or with other objects.
9. **Design challenge (p. 122):** use the properties of magnets to design a toy or game.
